## IV. Analysis

### A. Threshold: could a Travis County court even reach Zhao?

Two gates sit before any merits ruling in Austin:

1. **Consent jurisdiction while §14 stands.** A forum-selection clause consenting to Travis County generally supplies consent to personal jurisdiction. But if the clause is voided under Cal. Lab. Code §925 at Zhao's election, the Texas court must find **independent minimum contacts** — and per the library's practice note, *"Two short business trips to Austin and a contract with a Texas employer may or may not suffice."* (`tx_jurisdiction_note.md`). So even jurisdiction is a coin-flip once Zhao voids §14.
2. **Cross-forum reality.** The California Supreme Court has cautioned against California courts enjoining parties from litigating in other states' courts in parallel non-compete disputes (*Advanced Bionics*); the same interstate-comity sensitivities run in both directions, and the practice note warns that **an injunction entered in Texas would in any event face non-recognition in California on public-policy grounds for the non-compete.** An Austin order Zhao can ignore in Fremont is not enforcement; it is expense.

### B. Which law applies, and why

**Route 1 — Cal. Lab. Code §925 (dispositive on its own terms).** §925(a) forbids requiring an employee who "primarily resides and works in California," as a condition of employment, to agree to (1) adjudicate outside California a claim arising in California, or (2) be deprived of the substantive protection of California law as to a controversy arising in California. On this file, every §925(a) predicate is met: Zhao lived and worked fully remotely in Oakland for 2.5 years (two short Austin trips do not change "primarily"; client-confirmed), the Agreement was a standard form signed ~2 hours after receipt with no counsel — client-confirmed ("It's our standard form; engineers sign the same day. Nobody negotiates."), so the §925(e) individually-represented-by-counsel exception does not apply — and both the Travis County venue clause (§925(a)(1)) and the Texas choice-of-law clause that would displace §16600 (§925(a)(2)) are squarely covered. The consequence is severe for the strategy question: §925(b) makes such provisions **voidable by the employee**, and if voided, "the matter shall be adjudicated in California and California law shall govern the dispute." Zhao's resignation email states he has consulted a California attorney and "may void" the clauses — i.e., the election is waiting for him whenever he needs it. Meridian cannot rely on §14 outrunning an election Zhao can invoke at will. Meridian could still physically *file* in Austin (§925 does not itself divest Texas courts of jurisdiction), but the consent predicate for the forum would then be gone (§IV.A), and any merits ruling would be subject to the choice-of-law fight below.

**Route 2 — Restatement §187 (if §925 somehow were out of the way).** Both Texas and California apply Restatement §187: a choice of law is honored unless applying the chosen state's law would contravene a **fundamental policy** of a state with a **materially greater interest** in the issue (`restatement_187.md`). Texas adopted this in *DeSantis* (the Court refused to let a Florida clause displace Texas policy for a Texas employee); California adopted it in *Nedlloyd*, and *Application Group* applied it to hold that California's anti-non-compete policy is "fundamental" and that a Maryland employer's out-of-state choice-of-law clause did not control as to an employee connected to California.

Meridian's interest here is real (Texas employer, Austin venue recited, two trips) but the *issue* is the restraint of a specific human being's livelihood, and that person lived and worked the entire relevant period in California. Under *Application Group* — which is the closer analogue of the two — California's interest is materially greater and its §16600 policy fundamental, so §187(2)(b) routes the enforceability question to California law. We estimate ~70% probability a Texas court reaches that result (components in §IV.F); we do not treat that as a close call worth betting fee exposure on.

**What we cannot rely on and must not say.** The firm's 2024 bank memo recommended Travis County filings based on *Calderon v. Pacific Dynamics* and *Westbrook Systems v. Ng*. **Those decisions are not in the reference library, we cannot verify them, and we do not cite them.** The 2025 bank memo itself notes rehearing en banc was granted in *Westbrook* (argued Jan. 2025) — its current status is unknown to us. Whatever comfort those memos offered in the *Alvarez* matter cannot be carried into this memo or this exposure analysis.

### C. If California law applies — §8 is void, and its inclusion was unlawful

- Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600(a) voids "[e]very contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind," and §16600(b)(1) — added by SB 699, effective Jan. 1, 2024 — directs that the section be read broadly to void "any noncompete agreement in an employment context … **no matter how narrowly tailored**, that does not satisfy an exception in this chapter."
- *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen* (2008) expressly **rejected the Ninth Circuit's "narrow restraint" exception** and disapproved *Campbell* and *General Commercial Packaging*. Any instinct to argue that §8 "only" bars warehouse-AMR work — a sliver of engineering — is foreclosed; that is precisely the argument *Edwards* refused. Both superseded cases are flagged in `citations.json` as superseded and must not be cited (and if an adversary or a revived bank memo cites them, they are wrong).
- *Ixchel Pharma* (2020) confirms the strict rule is employment-specific: a rule of reason applies only to **business-to-business** restraints; in employment, non-competes are void. Meridian–Zhao is employment. There is no fact-based escape hatch in the library's authorities (no sale-of-business scenario, no statutory trade exception) — the library's §16600 text contains the statutory exceptions only by cross-reference ("[e]xcept as provided in this chapter"), and nothing suggests §8 fits one.
- Result: **§8 is void as applied to Zhao under California law.** §9's non-solicit and §10's confidentiality covenant are not voided by this logic (§10 plainly is fine; §9's fate under California law is **not verifiable from the library** — no authority on non-solicitation covenants is included, and we flag it as an open item rather than guess).
- **Separate statutory violation:** §16600.1(a) (AB 1076) declares it "unlawful to include a noncompete clause in an employment contract … that does not satisfy an exception in this chapter." Meridian *included* §8 in a California-resident engineer's contract on March 4, 2024. Under §16600.5(a) the void contract is unenforceable "regardless of where and when the contract was signed," and §16600.5(b) forbids enforcement attempts "regardless of whether the contract was signed and the employment was maintained outside of California" — language aimed at exactly this configuration (out-of-state employer, California employee). The violation is an act of unfair competition under §17200 (§16600.1(c)).

### D. If Texas law applied anyway — partial relief at best, and expensive

Assume, contrary to §IV.B, that a Travis County court applies Texas law (the ~30% branch):

- **Ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement.** Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.50(a) requires the covenant be ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement. Per *Sheshunoff*, an employer's promise to provide confidential information supports the covenant when that promise is **performed**, even in an at-will agreement (the older *Light* timing rule was modified by *Sheshunoff* — we do not rely on *Light*). Our Assumption A2 (actual delivery of confidential materials/training) is an **assumption** (client had no information); if nothing confidential or goodwill-related was actually delivered, the covenant may fail at step one.
- **Reasonableness — Meridian's burden.** §15.51(b) puts the §15.50(a) burden on the promisee when (as here) the agreement's primary purpose is personal services. The restraint is **24 months, nationwide, and the entire field** of Zhao's specialty (all AMR-for-warehouse-logistics companies). Texas law requires reasonable limitations as to time, geography, and scope that are no greater than necessary to protect goodwill or other business interests. As written, this is very likely overbroad: a California-based R&D engineer with two short Austin trips, restrained from his entire profession nationwide for two years, protects more than goodwill.
- **Reformation, not nullification — but no damages.** §15.51(c): if the limitations are unreasonable, the court **shall reform** and enforce as reformed, but **"may not award damages for breach before reformation."** So even Meridian's best Texas branch yields, prospectively, some narrower injunction — plausibly reduced duration/geography — and **zero damages** for the period Zhao has already worked at Kestrel (Oct. 13, 2026 → now). We cannot estimate required trade-secret-related showing from the library (see below). §15.52 makes the Act's framework exclusive.
- **Consideration varieties.** If Meridian granted Zhao equity, *Marsh USA* (stock-option consideration reasonably tied to goodwill) could strengthen the ancillary showing — we do not know whether he received equity (client has no information).

### E. Meridian's affirmative statutory exposure (not just defensiveness)

1. **Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600.5 (SB 699).** (d) Entering into a contract that is void under the chapter, or **attempting to enforce** one, is a civil violation. (e)(1) Zhao may sue for injunctive relief and actual damages; (e)(2) a **prevailing employee shall recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs** (mandatory). Filing a Travis County enforcement action on a contract void under §16600 is the paradigm "attempt to enforce." The one meaningful defense is the choice-of-law threshold (the contract is void "under this chapter" only if California law genuinely applies — see §IV.B, where we put that at ~70%+); and note the same "attempt" exposure can arise from **demand letters** as well as filings — the library's text contains no case law construing §16600.5's application to out-of-state filings or letters, so we flag that intensity-of-application question as open, while treating the risk as real.
2. **Cal. Lab. Code §925(c).** A court may award an employee **reasonable attorney's fees** for enforcing §925 rights (discretionary).
3. **AB 1076 / §16600.1.** The Timeline shows Meridian **never sent the required individualized written notices** — due **February 14, 2024** — to current and post-January 1, 2022 former California employees whose contracts contained non-compete clauses; the ticket was closed as "won't do" (LO-233; client-confirmed October 25, 2026). Each violation "constitutes an act of unfair competition within the meaning of Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 17200)."
   - **Zhao specifically:** his contract was signed March 4, 2024, *after* the February 14, 2024 deadline. Whether §16600.1(b)'s fixed deadline reaches employees hired after that date is **not resolved by the library's text** — we flag it as an open interpretive question rather than assert either way. What is clear: (i) *including* §8 in his contract was unlawful under §16600.1(a) the day it was signed; (ii) any *later* company-wide employees' notice obligations ran from that same deadline and were admittedly not met ("no notice was sent to California employees" — plural).
   - **Remediation arithmetic:** the number of current and post-2022-former California employees holding §8-type clauses is **unknown** (the client has no information; see §III). Exposure scales with that population; we cannot compute a dollar figure because the library does not include §17200 remedy amounts (penalties/restitution regimes are outside the library — labeled unverified).
4. **The FTC federal rule is unavailable.** The Non-Compete Clause Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 910) was **vacated nationwide** (*Ryan LLC v. FTC*, N.D. Tex. 2024; FTC dismissed its appeal and acceded to vacatur in September 2025 — the Rule never took effect). State law controls, as the 2025 bank memo itself recognizes. Any plan premised on federal help is dead.
