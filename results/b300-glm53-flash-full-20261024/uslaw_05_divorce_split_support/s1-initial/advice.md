# Advice to Wife — evaluating Husband's "equal halves" proposal

*Prepared for the client. All citations are to files in `library/`; when the library is silent on something, that is stated and labeled.*

## 1. Is the proposal fair? On paper — but not after tax.

Husband's schedule is arithmetically honest within its own four corners: total net estate 1,540,000 (house 1,100,000 − mortgage 400,000, plus 401(k) 400,000 + brokerage 300,000 + cash 100,000 + cars 40,000 = 1,540,000), and the two "halves" each total 770,000. The problem is what each of you actually keeps after taxes your assets carry, plus what his schedule **leaves out**.

**Your side, after tax (house valued at eventual sale at today's price):**

| Step | Amount |
|---|---:|
| House equity (1,100,000 − 400,000 mortgage) | 700,000 |
| Selling costs at eventual sale, 6% × 1,100,000 | −66,000 |
| Gain for tax: 1,034,000 realized − 300,000 carryover basis (§1041) = 734,000 | |
| §121 exclusion (single filer) −250,000 → taxable gain 484,000 | |
| Tax at 15% + 3.8% NIIT = 18.8% × 484,000 | −90,992 |
| Net house equity | **543,008** |
| Cash 50,000 + Volvo 20,000 | +70,000 |
| **Your after-tax total** | **613,008** |

**His side:**

| Step | Amount |
|---|---:|
| 401(k) 400,000 pre-tax, withdrawn at his assumed 24% marginal rate | 304,000 |
| Brokerage 300,000 − tax on gain 80,000 × 18.8% (15,040) | 284,960 |
| Cash 50,000 + Tacoma 20,000 | +70,000 |
| **His after-tax total** | **658,960** |

**Gap: Husband is 45,952 better off after tax** (613,008 vs 658,960) — about 6% of the estate. His side costs 111,040 in embedded tax (15.4% of what he takes); your side costs 156,992 (22.4% of the house equity: 66,000 selling costs + 90,992 tax). "50/50" in dollars is **not** 50/50 in value. Col. Rev. Stat. §14-10-113(1)(d) (per `columbia_equitable_distribution.md`) expressly requires the court to consider "the tax consequences of the division to each party," so this disparity is a direct statutory argument, not a stylistic quibble.

Two additional pressures on the numbers:

- **The concealed bonus.** His sworn affidavit (2026-08-30) says bonus "discretionary; none expected," but an HR letter (2026-03-02) written five months **before** the affidavit shows a 2026 target bonus of 45,000, half paid in September, with him at 112% of plan → expected 2026 bonus **50,400** (45,000 × 1.12). The September 15 pay stub corroborates: YTD gross 146,212 vs salary prorate 119,000 = **27,212 unexplained**, in the range of a 22,500–25,200 September installment. You have now told us he **always** gets a bonus and that his 2025 bonus was approximately **40,000** (your recollection — no 2025 employer documents are in the file, so the amount is flagged unverified, but recurrence itself is corroborated by the letter's plan language and the stub's unexplained excess). Rule 16.2(e) (per `columbia_equitable_distribution.md`) requires full disclosure of income "including bonuses and expected compensation," and authorizes **sanctions including an unequal division in your favor** and reopening of a judgment procured on a false affidavit. This is your leverage, and it is not theoretical — the false statement is in a sworn filing against documents from his own employer, and the "always gets a bonus" history is exactly what the guidelines' "expected or recurring bonuses" language captures (`columbia_child_support_guidelines.md`).
- **The maintenance tax claim is wrong.** The 2,000/month × 36 months is described as "deductible to Husband and taxable to Wife." For any instrument executed after December 31, 2018, that is no longer the law: alimony is neither deductible by the payor nor includible by the recipient (post-TCJA; `irc_1041_121_71.md`). Practically, that means the 72,000 comes to you **tax-free** — favorable to you; do not trade it for a "gross-up" that assumes it is taxable — but it also means he bears the full 72,000 with no offset, which is ground to push on the **36-month term**.

**Bottom line:** the structure (house to you, retirement account to him) can work for your goals, but only with (a) an equalization payment to you, and/or (b) the disclosure remedy. Don't sign "50/50" as drafted.

## 2. The house: a decision tree (built from your 2026-10 answer)

You want to stay 3–6 years, and you've said you don't know whether you can refinance the 400,000 mortgage on your income — and that if you can't, you'd sell. So the house plan is a tree, worked through in order:

**Step 1 — Resolve the refinance question *before* signing anything.** The arithmetic we can do: at an assumed 6.1% over 24 years the payment is **2,648/month** (400,000 × 0.61%/12 ÷ (1 − (1 + 0.61%/12)^−288); the rate is an assumption — your current note terms are not in the file). In **years 1–3**, against your 6,000 earnings + 2,000 maintenance + ~2,042 support ≈ 10,042/month, that is ≈ **26% of income** — and both support streams are court-ordered, which is income a lender can typically see on paper (whether and how a lender credits them is underwriting, outside this library — get **pre-approved now** and let the answer drive the tree). In **years 4–6** the cliff hits: maintenance (2,000 × 36 months) and child support (recalculated then, with the maintenance add/subtract out) both end, leaving the 2,648 payment against ≈ 6,000 of earnings — ≈ **44% of income**, before property tax, insurance, and upkeep, none of which are in the file. So even a successful refinance needs a years-4–6 plan (refinancing again, a shorter keep, or a sale at children's exit). **Lender qualification standards and pre-approval are outside the library and remain unknown** — nothing in the analysis below assumes they pass.

**Plan 1 — Keep via refinance (Alternative A in `division.json`; ends at ~636,008 you / ~635,960 him).** House to you; 400,000 refinanced into your name; he keeps 401(k)+brokerage and concedes **23,000** of liquid assets to equalize after-tax totals; quitclaim deed incident to the decree (no gain, §1041). Build the refinance into the decree as a **contingency**: a fixed window (e.g., 90 days) for you to close the refinance, with the fallback in Plan 2 or Plan 3 if the window lapses, and instruct that the deed passes only at/after the refinance unless you knowingly accept the joint-loan risk. The cost of this plan is deferral, not avoidance: your eventual solo sale still pays ≈91,000 tax on the 484,000 gain above your 250,000 exclusion.

**Plan 2 — Stay-then-sell with BOTH of you still on title — new "Alternative D," the fallback if you can't refinance.** Failing the refinance does **not** force you into either "sell now" or "sell later alone at a much bigger tax bill." There is a third structure: after the decree, **both of you remain on title**, the decree grants you **exclusive occupancy under the divorce instrument**, and the house is sold when the children leave (3–6 years). Reading the library's text of §121(b)(1) (each individual may exclude 250,000) with §121(d)(3)(A) (an individual is treated as using the property as a principal residence during any period of *ownership* while the spouse or former spouse is granted use under a *divorce instrument*), each former co-owner who is still an owner at sale may claim his or her own 250,000 — a combined **500,000 exclusion with no joint return and no marriage**. Arithmetic at today's values: gain 734,000 → 367,000 per co-owner (decree must fix the shares; analysis assumes equal) → each (367,000 − 250,000) × 18.8% = 21,996 → **total tax 43,992**, net 590,008, **each side 619,484** — exactly what a joint pre-decree sale produces (§1041/§121 per `irc_1041_121_71.md`; the (d)(3)(A) reading is our application of the library's excerpt, nothing in the library contradicts it). That is ≈ **47,000 better** than you owning alone at sale (tax 90,992 in the base case) while keeping the children in place. Mechanics that must be drafted: expense allocation (mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs) with a contribution ledger; an exit trigger (listing at children's exit, or either side may buy out at appraised value); and how sale proceeds divide. Trade-offs, flagged: his name stays on a 2,648/month obligation (leverage for cooperation, less control for you); future house value moves both of you together; expense-sharing terms shift real recoveries and are not priced here.

**Plan 3 — Sell now, before the decree, on a joint return (Alternative B; 619,484 each).** Identical numbers to Plan 2, but the children change homes immediately and you must stay married through the sale year (filing status fixed by December 31 status). It is the same economics as Plan 2 with worse fit to your goal — it belongs at the end of the tree, used only if co-ownership until exit is truly impossible.

**The one structure to refuse:** a decree that transfers the whole house to you, removes his name from nothing or from title with no refinance contingency, and leaves you to sell later as sole owner if things go wrong. That combination takes ~91,000 of tax instead of ~44,000, i.e., it gifts ≈47,000 to the estate's tax bill, while you carry the entire payment risk on a joint note. If a refinance is not in hand, the deed structure and the contingency *are* the deal.

**Watch liquidity under every plan:** the current proposal leaves you 50,000 cash against a 2,648/month obligation. Prefer cash/brokerage equalization over slicing the pre-tax 401(k): a QDRO slice rolls to an IRA and is taxed at *your* rate — unstated in the library, so its present value is unverified. Separately, put the **February 2027 bonus installment** (≈25,200) on the table: work performed during marriage (assumption flagged: consistent with the schedule treating his 401(k), which "accrued entirely during marriage," as marital; the library does not define marital-vs-separate boundaries).

## 3. What instruments are needed (none of this is "just sign the deed")

- **QDRO** (`qdro_72t.md`): the 401(k) is a qualified plan; any division must be made by a qualified domestic relations order. Today's proposal gives you none of it, but (a) any equalization slice from the 401(k) must be by QDRO; (b) a QDRO naming you alternate payee is the standard protection until every obligation under the decree is paid. Under a QDRO, distributions to you escape the §72(t) 10% penalty (§72(t)(2)(C)) and roll over to an IRA. Distinguish: an *IRA* is divided incident to divorce without a QDRO (§408(d)(6)); the 401(k) needs the order.
- **Refinance + quitclaim deed + decree order of operations:** refinance first, or decree with a deadline and a pay-off/purchase-price structure at refi (a promissory note if he carries your pay-off). Removing the joint note is as important as being on the deed: on a joint mortgage you are personally liable for 2,648/month whether or not you live there.
- **Form 8332** (`filing_status_dependents.md`): only sign it if you *choose* to release dependency years to him — see §5. Its default absence preserves the credits for you.
- **Corrected financial affidavit / Rule 16.2(e) motion:** the nondisclosure remedy runs through his corrected affidavit, disclosure of the bonus plan document, and (if he stonewalls) the sanctions/reopening procedure.

## 5. Children as dependents, and your filing status

- Filing status is fixed by your marital status on December 31 (`filing_status_dependents.md`). If the decree enters on or before that date, you file as single or **head of household**. Head of household requires a qualifying child living with you more than half the year and that you pay more than half the cost of the home — with ~70% of nights and the house transferring to you, that is likely, though fact-dependent once the living arrangements are settled.
- **§152(e): you (the custodial parent) claim the children unless you release the claim on Form 8332.** His "alternating years" plan requires you to sign a release in each of his years. The release moves the **child tax credit** to him for the released years, but cannot move head-of-household status, EITC, or the dependent-care credit — those stay with you regardless. Recommended position: don't sign Form 8332 at all; if you need trade value, release only the dependency/CTC on Form 8332 for specific years, and never the credits that legally can't move anyway.
