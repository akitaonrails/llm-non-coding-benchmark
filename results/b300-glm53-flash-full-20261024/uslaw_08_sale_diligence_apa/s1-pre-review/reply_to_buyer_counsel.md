## 1. Schedule 3.3 / consents

- **Cascade Builders framework agreement (11%)** — assignment to a successor to substantially all assets is permitted on notice; included for completeness as a notice item, not a consent.
- **Twenty-two residential contracts (<$40k each)** — silent on assignment; no consent changes needed on the authority we rely on (contract rights assignable absent language to the contrary; obligations require consent only if personal performance has been made a condition — see `library/anti_assignment_change_of_control.md`). ## 2. WARN

Confirmed **no WARN exposure** on this headcount, and the reason is structural rather than a close call:

- WARN reaches employers with **100 or more employees** (excluding part-time) and covers plant closings/mass layoffs affecting 50+ employees at a single site (29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(1) as summarized in `library/warn_act.md`; primary/affiliates exclusion in 20 C.F.R. §639.3).
- The Brightline census shows **14 W-2 employees** (7 shop, 4 installers, 3 office), no union, single site.
- Even if your client elects to treat the 9 "independent installers" as employees for WARN counting (readers of our correspondence will see that classification is separately addressed on the disclosure side), the count is 14 + 9 = **23 — well under 100**. A business this size is not a covered employer, and there is no WARN duty even if Buyer ultimately hires fewer than all of them.
- Texas has no mini-WARN statute; the county-based state analog is likewise not a factor at this headcount.

So your client may proceed on its planned staffing decisions free of WARN notice obligations for this target. (We note for completeness only: nothing here should be confused with the *other* heads of exposure around the installers — decoupled from WARN, those are addressed in ¶3 and the indemnity scheduling.)

## 3. §7.4(c) — our position

We cannot accept §7.4(c) as drafted ("knowledge acquired (or capable of being acquired) at any time, whether before or after the execution of this Agreement"). That is the broadest pro-sandbagging formulation, and it is not a reasonable simplification **in this transaction**: it operates together with other provisions of your draft to make Seller the insurer of any issue Buyer learns about anywhere, any time — including on the §4.12 classification facts we are scheduling. Our authority on practice confirms the alternative approaches; a seller who discloses a known item and then signs a clean rep under a pro-sandbagging clause has simply pre-paid an indemnity for that item, which is not anyone's intent here (`library/sandbagging.md`, citing Delaware/Texas practice and *Akorn v. Fresenius* (Del. Ch. 2018) (dicta)).

Our counter is attached as a redline; in substance:

- **§7.4(c)** becomes an anti-sandbagging provision: Buyer's recovery is not affected by Buyer's knowledge, but is precluded (outside Fundamental Representations and Buyer's knowing fraud, and outside scheduled matters, where recovery runs through the newly-added §7.1A Special Indemnity) where Buyer had actual knowledge of the breach at or prior to Closing.
- **§4.12** is qualified by a new **Schedule 4.12** (the nine installers) and paired with a **§7.1A Special Indemnity capped at $275,000** — sized to the arithmetic your client will want: indicative exposure brackets $228,031–$302,619 (employer FICA at an assumed 7.65% on $612k of annual installer pay plus overtime premiums at 3.4-year average tenure; we will share the calculation schedule on request, with the flagged assumptions visible). A capped special indemnity is the market-typical answer where a buyer wants certainty on a specific known item; a full-price-cap rep with statute-of-limitations-plus survival for an operational matter like this is not the market answer (`library/baskets_caps.md`).
- **§7.4(b)**: we pull §4.12 back under the general 10% cap, and cap the classification exposure specifically through §7.1A. Buyer keeps Fundamental Reps at full price cap. Nothing above touches Buyer's fraud or Buyer's right to be told the truth through schedules.
- Closing mechanics note (not a numbered section): the spray-booth reapplication window (30–60 days) is on everyone's critical path. Signing in two weeks works if Buyer is flexible on the closing date; we'd propose a target closing of approximately 60 days post-signing.

The other redlined provisions (§§2.4, 3.3, 8.2) are self-executing on their face; happy to walk through them. In particular, on §2.4: "EBITDA ... determined by Buyer under Buyer's accounting policies" gives your client both the yardstick and the scoreboard — we've replaced it with a defined-Earn-Out structure with ordinary-course covenant, audit and independent-accountant resolution, and acceleration, the protections conventionally found in these provisions (`library/earnout_definitions.md`). On §8.2: 7 years/500 miles invites reformation, which serves neither side — our counter is 3 years / 75-mile radius (see `library/tx_noncompete_sale_of_business.md`).

We can get you a full marked draft of Schedule 3.3 and Schedule 4.12 this week. Happy to get on a call after your client has reviewed.

## Addendum — response to "§7.4(c) is market, every deal has it" and "§4.12 is a plain-vanilla compliance rep"

Received via our client; responding on the merits.

### 1. "Is market, every deal has it"

Two points, without heat:

- **On market practice:** the authorities we rely on treat this as a live choice, not a universal: both pro-sandbagging and anti-sandbagging provisions are described as market alternatives (`library/sandbagging.md`, citing ABA Private Target Deal Points Study). We do not need to win the philosophy fight — see the package below, which lets Buyer keep the clause.
- **On the particular wording:** even the aggressive form our authority describes turns on buyer knowledge *before signing or closing*. Your draft — knowledge "acquired (or capable of being acquired) **at any time**, whether before or after the execution of this Agreement" — reaches matters discovered after execution, which goes beyond the formulation we find described. Whatever is "market" for pre-signing knowledge is not necessarily market for that open-ended extension, and we are not persuaded it is common.

### 2. "§4.12 is vanilla — if Ray's contractors are fine, what's the concern?"

We agree the rep is vanilla. The premise is not. Buyer's own diligence file (data room §2.2) says the nine "independent installers": work exclusively for Brightline, drive Brightline trucks, use shop tools, are scheduled daily by the shop foreman, are paid hourly ($34–41), and average ~3.4 years of tenure, at 45–50 hours in busy season paid straight time. Under the common-law control test, that census describes employees, not contractors, and the same authority expressly notes "industry practice" is no defense (`library/worker_classification_tx_irs.md`, IRS Rev. Rul. 87-41; Treas. Reg. §31.3121(d)-1).

So the concern is concrete: as drafted, §4.12 is a knowingly false statement, §7.4(b) caps it at the full Purchase Price, and §7.4(c) preserves the claim even though Buyer knows the facts. Indicative arithmetic (assumptions flagged in our redline): employer FICA at an upper-bound 7.65% on $612,000 of annual installer pay = $46,818/yr; overtime premiums $20,250–$42,188/yr; both over 3.4-year average tenure → indicative bracket **$228,031–$302,619**. Signing the clean rep turns that into exposure up to the full price, far beyond the $310,000 escrow. Vanilla practice — and both sides' deal points studies say the same — is that a known issue goes on the schedule or gets a sized special indemnity (`library/sandbagging.md` (seller protections (ii)–(iii)); `library/baskets_caps.md`: "a specific operational rep (like worker classification) carved out to a full-price cap is off-market and should be resisted or replaced with a sized special indemnity").

### 3. The pragmatic package — either option gets this signed

- **Option A (our redline, prior letter):** anti-sandbagging §7.4(c) with Fundamental-Rep and fraud carve-outs; classification boxed by Schedule 4.12 + §7.1A ($275,000 cap); §4.12 off the full-price carve-out.
- **Option B (concession, offered here):** Buyer keeps its §7.4(c) **verbatim** for every unscheduled matter, and the deal adds only: (i) the new **Schedule 4.12** listing the nine installers; (ii) the **§7.1A Special Indemnity capped at $275,000** as Buyer's exclusive remedy for the scheduled classification matters (buyer's knowing fraud and Fundamental-Rep breaches excepted); and (iii) §4.12 removed from the §7.4(b) full-price carve-out. Net effect: Buyer keeps its "market" clause intact for everything else; the one known item is priced and capped — $275,000, ~8.9% of the inferred $3.1M price, inside the $310,000 escrow — and the seller is not reciting a falsehood he will pay for at the buyer's option.

**The one combination we cannot sign into the clean rep:** a rep we know is false + no schedule + full-price cap + post-signing knowledge coverage. On your draft exactly as received, our advice to our client will be not to sign — and we would rather close the deal than grandstand over it, which is why Option B asks you to give up almost nothing you have said you need.

Mechanics unchanged from our prior letter: the Schedule 3.3 counter is ready to deliver; your Schedule 3.1(c) remains outstanding; and the spray-booth reapplication (30–60 days) sets the closing calendar whatever we do at signing. If the package is agreed, signing next week is realistic.
