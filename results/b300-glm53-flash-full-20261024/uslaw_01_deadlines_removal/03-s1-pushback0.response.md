## The friend's rule is the rule for *corporations* — and it expressly does not apply to an LLC

"Different states = diversity" is correct as far as it goes. But an entity's citizenship depends on **what kind of entity it is**:

- **Corporations** get the two-prong test your friend described: citizen of the state of incorporation **plus** the state of its principal place of business (§1332(c)(1)). Under *that* test, he'd be right: Delaware would count, Caleb wouldn't — "the company isn't him" is true for corporations.
- **LLCs use a different test**: a limited liability company "is a citizen of **every state of which its owners/members are citizens**; the §1332(c)(1) corporate test … **does not apply**" (*Johnson v. Columbia Properties Anchorage, LP* (9th Cir.); *Carden v. Arkoma Associates* (U.S.)). *Johnson*'s words: "**like a partnership, an LLC is a citizen of every state of which its owners/members are citizens.**"

And it's not us calling Harbor Street an LLC — the complaint does: "**Harbor Street Bakery LLC** is a Delaware LLC" (complaint ¶2). So the corporate analysis never reaches us.

## "The company isn't him" — that's exactly the intuition the Supreme Court rejected for LLCs

For an LLC there is **no separate company citizenship to look at** — the company's citizenship *is* its members' citizenships, one by one, with no weighting.
