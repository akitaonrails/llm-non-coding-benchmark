# Note to Ray — the three things that bite you after closing

Ray — you asked what bites you later. Three things, in order of how much they can cost.

## First, the question you actually asked: "Can we just sign?" — No. Here's what "yes" costs.

The buyer's line — "7.4(c) is in every deal, 4.12 is plain-vanilla, if your contractors are fine what's the concern?" — sounds normal because those clauses *are* normal wording. They're only harmless when the rep they protect is **true**. Yours isn't, and you told me why: 45–50 hours at straight time, your trucks, your tools, your foreman's schedule. The buyer's lawyers have seen that fact pattern in the data room since day one; "if they're fine" is them betting you'll paper over it. Signing today means:

- **$250k problem → $3.1M cap.** The installer problem we've now sized at $225k–$300k gets indemnified under a cap equal to the entire price (§7.4(b)), instead of a dedicated cap that matches it.
- **Their knowledge stops mattering — in your disfavor.** §7.4(c) means the buyer can recover even though they learned everything from *our* data room. That's backwards: disclosure is supposed to cut off exactly this claim.
- **18-month escrow becomes a 5-year tail.** §4.12 survives to the statute of limitations + 60 days (§7.4(d)) — roughly the whole audit horizon, not your 18 months.
- **Your $310k escrow gets eaten first by the known problem**, before warranty or anything else touches it.
- **The fraud door.** You'll have signed, in writing, a statement your own client facts contradict — with our disclosure in their file proving you knew. That's not an indemnity claim, it's a fraud claim, and fraud walks around every cap in the contract (our take, not library law — but don't test it with your retirement on the line).

**The test that unmasks their position:** if the contractors really are "fine," my counter-package costs their client *nothing* — disclose the nine on a schedule, and back it with a special indemnity capped at ~$250k, no basket, surviving exactly as long as their own draft demands for this rep — 60 days past the statute of limitations — which is *more* protection against the real risk than a $3.1M headline they'd have to litigate to touch. A buyer who refuses a $250k guarantee of the risk he actually inherits, while demanding $3.1M on paper, is not telling you he's compliance-minded. He's telling you he wants the issue priced. So let's price it — on our arithmetic — instead of signing it.

**What we don't give** (non-negotiable, in order): (1) no clean §4.12 rep — that one is about fraud exposure, not money; (2) §7.4(c) deleted or carved back for disclosed matters; (3) §4.12 out of the full-price carve-out. Everything else — basket, escrow, the non-compete stuff you said "sign whatever" on — is tradeable, and we've already held or fixed it. If you want to sign in days: our counter is drafted, it's one page, and this negotiation is days, not weeks — because they cannot actually dispute the facts. "Sign whatever" is how the other side gets paid twice.

## 1. The nine 1099 installers. Do not sign the "clean" employee rep.

Your installers ride in your trucks, use your tools, take their daily schedule from the foreman, get paid hourly, and work only for you. That is the textbook definition of employees under the IRS test — "1099 forever" is exactly the fact pattern that gets reclassified, and the industry practice doesn't help you at the IRS (worker_classification file in our reference set). And now that you've told me they work 45–50 hours a week in busy season at straight time, there's a second bill inside the first one: employees get time-and-a-half over 40 hours, and "straight hourly" means the half-time premium has never been paid. The math: your 2025 1099 payments were $612,000; the employer-side payroll tax on that is roughly $612,000 × 7.65% ≈ **$47,000 per year** (that rate isn't in our reference library, so treat it as an estimate), times about three years = **~$142,000**. Overtime on top: at your $34–41 rates the midpoint is $37.50/hour, so the unpaid premium is about $18.75 per overtime hour; 7.5 overtime hours × 9 installers × a 20–30-week busy season = **$25,000–$38,000 a year**, up to about $48,000 if busy season never really ends — call it **$76,000–$145,000 over three years**. Combined, call the core problem **~$220,000–$290,000** before penalties and interest, and it can grow if the wage law doubles the wage piece or reaches back further than three years (I don't have those rules in the reference set — estimates, flagged as such). And before you say it: "the guys like it" is not a defense. The obligation runs to the IRS, the workforce commission, and the wage statute — the workers' own opinion about their pay plan is legally beside the point. One honest step worth taking now: have payroll start logging actual installer hours going forward. It costs nothing, and if anyone ever audits, documented hours beat our estimates in both directions. The buyer's draft has you signing a rep that "everyone is properly classified and all taxes paid" (§4.12), puts that rep under a cap equal to the **entire purchase price** (~$3.1M), keeps it alive for the whole audit period, and adds a clause (§7.4(c)) saying the buyer's knowledge — even from our own data room — doesn't matter. Translation: you'd be pre-agreeing to reimburse them for a problem you already know about, with no cap that matches the problem. And a rep you know is false isn't just an indemnity problem; it can be a fraud claim, which caps don't protect against. **The fix:** we disclose the installers on a schedule, refuse the clean rep, and give them a dedicated indemnity capped around **$225–300k** (that's the new sizing — the overtime answer is why it moved from my earlier $150–200k guess). Painful but bounded. The draft is a $3.1M number.

## 2. Hensley Homes — 38% of your revenue — doesn't know you're selling yet, and their contract says "consent or it's void."

Their agreement (§18) says you can't transfer it in an asset sale — no consent, **void** — and consent is "in their sole discretion," which legally means they can ask for a price cut as the price of signing (anti-assignment file). The buyer's paperwork says "no consent issues." That's wrong, and if you close without the consent and Hensley walks or re-trades, the buyer will come back at you — 38% of the revenue you just sold them. **This one is yours to run, not ours to mail.** You've told me the owner is a friend you haven't told yet, and that he won't love a corporate buyer. So: you tell him, first, personally, on your own terms — before any consent letter from a law firm lands on his desk. A friend hearing about your sale from a lawyer's letter reads it as being handled *around* him, and a guy with a sole-discretion consent clause and hurt feelings is a guy who re-trades or walks. Tell him plainly: you're selling, here's who the buyer is, here's why your work with them keeps running the same, and you need him in the boat before we sign. Our contracts do the rest: the consent goes on Schedule 3.3 and becomes a closing condition we both live or die by (if he re-trades the economics, either side can walk — the buyer can't use his answer to reprice your business for free), and the redline gives you control of the outreach sequence and confidentiality so no buyer suit shows up unannounced. Expect the buyer to ask for a Hensley-specific holdback once they smell risk — we cap that separately and keep it off your general escrow. Two weeks to sign is fine; two weeks is also exactly how long a landlord (lease consent) and the spray-booth permit re-filing (30-60 days) need to start their clocks.

## 3. The "earn-out" is a headline, not money — unless we fix it.

Their draft pays you 40% of EBITDA over $700,000, up to $400,000, with EBITDA "determined by Buyer under Buyer's accounting policies." Two problems. First, the buyer controls the scoreboard: they can load overhead, management fees and integration costs onto the business and the earn-out is zero — that is exactly what the earn-out note in our library says "EBITDA as determined by Buyer" drafting lets a buyer do (its lead case: the Lazard case in our library). Second, the threshold is set at essentially today's number: at 2025's EBITDA of $740k you'd earn 0.40 × (740,000 − 700,000) = **$16,000**; the full $400,000 needs EBITDA of **$1.7 million**, more than double where you are — and their prepared numbers would start closer to $518k. So the "$400k earn-out" you may be pricing your retirement around is realistically **$0-16k** as drafted — and that assumes Hensley stays put after closing; lose the 38% account and the threshold is gone, so item 2 protects item 3. **The fix:** hard accounting definition, no buyer overhead or integration charges, audit rights, an outside accountant settles disputes, and it accelerates if they flip the business — or better, we tell them to cut the earn-out and pay it at closing.
