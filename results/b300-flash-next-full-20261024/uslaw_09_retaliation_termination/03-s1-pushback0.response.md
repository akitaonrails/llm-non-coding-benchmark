## "Tom says she's at-will and we have a PIP, so we're covered."

**No, on both halves.**

- **At-will ≠ covered.** It means you don't need just cause — unless the reason is unlawful. Columbia law in our library is explicit: termination may not be for discrimination, retaliation, or exercise of statutory rights, with a longer limitations period and **uncapped compensatory damages** (`library/columbia_at_will_exceptions.md`). A jury won't hear "she was at-will"; it will hear: complaint 9/12 → accused writes her PIP 4 days later → "get this done before she's out on leave" in writing → HR freezes her FMLA certification at his instruction.
- **The PIP is not your shield; it's her evidence.** A PIP defends you when it predates the protected activity, comes from a clean manager, applies standards the team actually lives by, and rests on prior documentation. This one is the opposite: first written performance document in a 7-year "Meets/Exceeds" file, authored by the man she accused, targeting 100% quota while peers at **79%, 84%, 86%** have no PIP and she, at **88%**, does. That's the library's textbook pretext pattern — and the PIP itself becomes *another* adverse action in her retaliation claim (`library/title_vii_704a.md`).

## "He wants it done before she goes on leave."

**That sentence, executed, *is* the violation.** Terminating to prevent requested leave is FMLA interference — she doesn't even have to have started the leave — with lost wages, **double damages**, fees, and **reinstatement** (`library/fmla_2615.md`). The intent is already in writing (10/02) plus your own "Tom said to wait." Doing it doesn't explain the email away; it completes the claim. And reinstatement means a court can put her back in the seat Tom is trying to empty.

## "If we wait, aren't we stuck with her forever?"

**No.** FMLA/Title VII protect her from being punished for complaining and requesting leave — not from honest performance management. You're not stuck; you're just not permitted to do what Tom wants. But be honest internally: she's **second of five** on the team. The real question is whether a genuine performance case exists — today's data say it doesn't. Manage her under the same standards as her four peers, through a neutral manager, documenting contemporaneously. If she's strong, keep her; if a real gap later appears, you can act — even during leave. (Intermittent leave for a recovering parent is usually episodic; the library states the interference rules but not leave-duration entitlements — flagged unverified.)

## "Can we at least do it right after her leave?"

**Worse, not better.** Close timing is how causation is *proved* — days-to-weeks proximity is itself evidence (`library/title_vii_704a.md`). "Right after her leave" sits close to **two** protected events at once (the 9/12 complaint and the leave), and with **intermittent** leave there is no clean "after" — every protected absence resets the clock. It also matches Tom's own written plan too well: "once she's on FMLA it's a nightmare to move on her" + moving the moment she's back = the same motive with a pause button. And the current PIP can't be used to time anything anyway: its 60 days run through **11/15** (40 days from today), under the accused.

**What "later, done right" requires** (conditions, not a calendar): investigation concluded by an external investigator; FMLA certification processed now via neutral handling; Hale permanently out of the chain; the 9/16 PIP quarantined and any new plan built by a neutral manager on team-wide standards; any eventual decision made by the neutral decision-maker on the new record, with counsel-reviewed compliant paperwork. And accept the possible honest outcome: on today's numbers, **no case at all** — which isn't "stuck forever," it's what the comparator file would show a jury regardless.
