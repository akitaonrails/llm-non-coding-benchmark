## Lena's follow-up questions, answered (10/06)

**"Tom says she's at-will and we have a PIP, so we're covered." He is wrong, on both halves.**

- **At-will doesn't mean "covered" — it means "no reason required, unless the reason is unlawful."** Columbia law is explicit: employment is at will, *but termination may not be for an unlawful reason — discrimination, retaliation, or exercise of statutory rights* (`library/columbia_at_will_exceptions.md`). At-will status answers "do we need just cause?" It answers nothing about *why*. A jury will never be told "she was at-will"; it will be told: complaint on 9/12, accused wrote her PIP on 9/16, accused said "get this done before she's out on leave" on 10/02, HR froze her FMLA certification at his direction. State law adds a longer limitations period and **uncapped compensatory damages** on top.
- **A PIP only protects you when it is credible — this one is the plaintiff's evidence.** A defensible PIP predates the protected activity, comes from a manager with no stake, applies standards the team actually lives by, and sits on top of contemporaneous documentation. This PIP has none of that: it arrived **4 days after** the complaint, **written by the accused** (confirmed by you on 10/06), the **first written performance document in a seven-year file** of "Meets/Exceeds," targeting 100% quota when 4 of the 5 team members (all but Lind) finished the year below that mark, while three *lower-performing* peers (79%, 84%, 86%) have no PIP. Under the library standard that is the textbook *pretext* pattern — and the PIP isn't just the (weak) justification; it may itself be a separate **adverse action** adding to the retaliation claim (`library/title_vii_704a.md` cites *Burlington Northern v. White*; note: the library does not state that decision's materiality standard, so treat the PIP's standalone actionability as **[UNVERIFIED]** rather than cite it as settled).

**"He wants it done before she goes on leave." That sentence, if acted on, IS the violation.**

- Terminating an employee to prevent her from taking requested leave is **interference** under 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a) — she doesn't even have to have started the leave (`library/fmla_2615.md`). The intent is already in writing (the 10/02 email) plus your own confirmation that the certification sat because "Tom said to wait." Executing the plan doesn't prove the email innocent; it completes the claim — lost wages, **liquidated (double) damages**, fees, and **reinstatement** as remedies. Yes, reinstatement: a court can put her back in the seat Tom is trying to empty.

**"If we wait, aren't we just stuck with her forever?" No. You aren't stuck — you're just not allowed to do what Tom wants.**

- FMLA and Title VII protect her from being punished **for requesting leave and for complaining**. They do not grant immunity from honest performance management. Nothing in the library says you can ever *ever* terminate an employee on or after leave — what you cannot do is let leave or the complaint be a factor, and right now you cannot show they aren't (`library/fmla_2615.md`, 29 C.F.R. § 825.220(c); `library/title_vii_704a.md`).
- The honest internal answer, though: she's at **88%, second of five** on the team. The real question isn't "how do we get rid of her," it's "do we actually have a performance case?" Today's data say no. Manage her honestly — same standards as the four peers, neutral manager (not Hale), contemporaneous documentation — and the law takes care of itself: if she's strong, keep the strong employee; if a genuine gap later appears and is documented like it was for peers, you can act then, even during leave.
- Practical note: an **intermittent** leave for a recovering parent is typically episodic, not a months-long absence. (The library states the interference rules and remedies but does not specify leave-duration entitlements — **[UNVERIFIED]**, so we flag it rather than cite it.)

**"Can we at least do it right after her leave?" That timing choice would manufacture new evidence against you.**

- Causation in these cases is *proved by* close timing — "very close temporal proximity (days to a few weeks)" between protected activity and adverse action is itself evidence (`library/title_vii_704a.md`). Terminating immediately upon her return is proximity to **two** protected events at once: the 9/12 complaint and the leave she took.
- With **intermittent** FMLA leave there is no clean "after" — every protected absence episode is protected activity. A termination weeks after her last protected absence re-creates the same days-to-weeks pattern each time.
- It also matches Tom's own written plan too well. "Once she's on FMLA it's a nightmare to move on her" reads as: the leave, not the performance, drove the timing — so "wait out the nightmare, then move" is the same unlawful motive with a pause button. A jury gets the narrative in Tom's own words, and your 10/06 admissions ("Tom wrote the PIP and Tom wants her out"; "Tom said to wait") fill in the rest.
- And the current PIP can't be used to time it: the 60-day plan opened 9/16 runs through **11/15** — 40 days out as of the 10/06 request date these materials are anchored to — under the accused, built on targets the team doesn't meet. "Completing" it launders nothing; it extends the taint.

**What "later, done right" would actually require (conditions precedent, not a calendar):**

1. Harassment investigation, run externally, **concluded** — with corrective action if substantiated (`library/harassment_investigation_duty.md`).
2. FMLA certification processed now through neutral handling; the "wait" instruction rescinded; leave approved/designated if eligible.
