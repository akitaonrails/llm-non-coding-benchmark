## What survives, in one paragraph

Section 7.3 eliminates the Manager's fiduciary duties of care and loyalty to the
fullest extent the Act allows, and 6 Del. C. §18-1101(c) makes that elimination
enforceable — so the claim the client wants to bring ("breach of fiduciary
duty") is the one claim that does not survive. What survives is everything the
contract itself promised and everything the implied covenant protects, because
§18-1101(c)'s proviso and §7.3's own carve-out preserve the implied contractual
covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and §18-1101(e) bars exculpation for
its bad-faith violation. Concretely: §5.4's affiliate-transaction conditions
(arm's-length terms plus written disclosure within 30 days for anything over
$50,000/yr) and §6.3's capital-call mechanics (bona fide purpose stated in the
notice, service **by certified mail and email**, 30 days' lead, dilution only on
fair value determined **in good faith**) are contractual promises that §7.3 did
not touch. Those are the case.

## Lead with the capital call, not the fees

The instinct is to lead with the looting story — $1,050,000 of fees to Bram's
wholly owned Castellan Asset Services with no written disclosure, ever. It is
the most sympathetic narrative and it is the weaker vehicle, for three reasons.
It is derivative: the harmed party is the Company, and the recovery goes to the
Company, so Imani captures only her percentage of it (*Tooley*,
library/tooley_direct_derivative.md) — and her percentage is exactly what is in
dispute. Its damages measure needs a market fee benchmark we do not have. And
the Company will still argue limitations as to the 2022 and 2023 payments
($375,000), which forces Imani to win the tolling fight before she can win the
fee claim — a fight she is now well positioned to win (below), but a fight
first.

The capital call is the stronger opening, because it is **direct** (*Tooley*:
wrongful dilution of the member's own interest), it is **fresh** (2026-08-20
notice, 2026-09-25 dilution), and it is **documentary** — the Manager's own
produced minutes record the defects.

1. **Notice mechanics.** §6.3 requires written notice delivered "by certified
   mail and email at least 30 days before the due date." Imani got a text message
   and a PDF, nothing else — **[CONFIRMED]** no certified letter was ever
   received, and the email went to an address on the Company's own file that has
   been dead since 2019. The 31-day lead time (2026-08-20 to 2026-09-20) cleared
   the 30-day floor, so timing is not the issue — delivery is. The library is
   explicit that the issuer must strictly comply with the agreement's notice
   mechanics and that a call failing the specified delivery method **does not
   trigger dilution** (*Zimmerman v. Crothall*,
   library/capital_call_notice_cases.md). No dilution means she is still an 18%
   Member and §9.2 never gave anyone the right to buy her out at 6.1%. Two
   defenses the confirmation puts on the table, addressed under Risks: she read
   the text (prejudice), and she stayed silent because she "assumed it wasn't
   real notice" (acquiescence).

2. **Purpose.** §6.3 requires "a bona fide Company purpose set forth in the call
   notice." The notice says "working capital and opportunities." Nothing in the
   file shows the Company needed $1.5M or had a project — and the client has no
   information either way (explicit unknown). A call lacking the stated bona
   fide purpose does not trigger dilution (same source). This claim needs
   discovery — it is medium, not high, on today's record.

3. **The valuation is arithmetically impossible, and that is the best fact in
   the file.** The Manager's own minutes record that Bram funded $1,230,000
   **and** Imani's $270,000 share, so the open question is not who wrote the
   check but how her $270,000 was booked. Take the Manager's own 2026-09-25
   "fair value" of $7.2M pre-money and credit Bram the full $1.5M he funded:
   Imani is 1,296,000 / 8,700,000 = **14.90%**. Exclude her $270,000 from the
   equity denominator entirely (booked as debt or an advance, not contributed
   capital) and credit her nothing: 1,296,000 / 8,430,000 = **15.37%**. Use
   the broker's $9.8M: **15.61%**. None of these is 6.1%, and no positive fair
   value produces 6.1% if her $270,000 was instead credited to Imani — that
   convention leaves her at exactly 18%. Reverse-solving
   (0.18F + cI)/(F + cB + cI) = 0.061, the 6.1% requires a pre-money value of
   **$630,504** (only $1.23M of contributed capital enters the denominator;
   her $270,000 booked outside equity, nothing credited to her) or
   **$768,908** (the recomputation credits Bram with the full $1.5M and Imani
   with nothing) — an **89%–91% discount to his own contemporaneous valuation** and
   92%–94% below the broker's. A fair value committed to the Manager's "good faith" is
   reviewable under the implied covenant for arbitrariness or bad faith —
   expressly including "a value far below a contemporaneous independent
   indication" (library/capital_call_notice_cases.md) — and *Dieckman v. Regency
   GP LP* (library/implied_covenant_cases.md) supplies the companion holding:
   contractual discretion must be exercised honestly and not arbitrarily or in
   bad faith so as to deprive the counterparty of the fruits of the bargain,
   even where duties are exculpated. This is
   the claim the §7.3 waiver cannot reach: the covenant survives by force of
   §18-1101(c)'s proviso and by §7.3's own text, and §18-1101(e) bars eliminating
   liability for a bad-faith violation of it.

One caution on that arithmetic, stated plainly because the contract says not to
force anything to balance: we have not seen Bram's computation, only his
headline — and the client has no way to tell us how he got 6.1% (explicit
unknown). If he produces a coherent derivation, the claim narrows to the value
input rather than the method — which is still an 89%–91% discount against his
own $7.2M. Either way
the record will have to show what he actually booked for her $270,000: loan,
credited contribution, or simply taken interest.

## Fee claims — keep them, and now expect the full $1,050,000

The fixed asset-management fees paid 2022-09-01 through 2026-09-01 total
180,000 + 195,000 + 210,000 + 225,000 + 240,000 = **$1,050,000**, every year far
above §5.4's $50,000 disclosure trigger, with no written disclosure found in the
file and none claimed to exist. The limitations picture has now materially
improved. **[CONFIRMED]**: Imani first saw the affiliate fees in 2026, when a
books-and-records demand finally produced the bank statements; until then she
received one-page summaries labeled "management expenses" that named no payee
and disclosed no affiliate relationship. The library states that a breached
contractual disclosure duty — it names §5.4's 30-day notice specifically —
supports concealment-based tolling as to undisclosed fees
(library/del_limitations_tolling.md), and the "management expenses" label is
more than silence: describing affiliate payments in a way that conceals their
affiliate character is the kind of affirmative act Delaware's fraudulent-
concealment line looks at (*Wal-Mart v. AIG Life*, same source). Primary
position: the three years run from the 2026 production and **all $1,050,000 is
live**. Risk scenario: if the court holds the summaries disclosed enough of the
injury to start the clock, 2022 and 2023 ($375,000) drop and $675,000 survives —
which is still why the fee counts earn their place in the complaint. Equitable
tolling on a fiduciary rationale remains unavailable, since §7.3 eliminated the
very duties that rationale needs; the concealment theory carries these counts
alone. The exact 2026 production date is an explicit unknown to pin down at
drafting.

One number to keep in the negotiation room: at 18%, Imani's indirect share of a
full derivative fee recovery is 0.18 × $1,050,000 = **$189,000** — within $100
of the $189,100 buyout offer. As framed, Bram is proposing to buy her out for
roughly what her own share of the undisclosed fees he paid himself would be
worth to her. It is arithmetic, not a legal argument, but it reframes the offer.

Two secondary points worth having but not worth pleading as separate counts. The
escalator does not match the authorizing resolution: 8%/yr compounding on
$180,000 gives 180,000 / 194,400 / 209,952 / 226,748 / 244,888, but the bank file
shows 180,000 / 195,000 / 210,000 / 225,000 / 240,000 — flat +$15,000/yr, which
by 2026 is $4,888 *under* the authorized amount. Immaterial in dollars, useful
as proof that the affiliate's compensation was administered off-book rather than
per the resolution. And the 6%-of-gross-rents management fee on top of the fixed
fee is **unquantifiable on this file** — the 2026 bank-statement production
apparently did not quantify the monthly draws and no rent roll exists in the
file. One thing *can* be computed without the rent roll: §5.4's disclosure
trigger bites once annualized gross rents exceed $50,000 / 0.06 =
**$833,333/yr**, which for a property supporting a $14.5M enterprise value is
near certain. So the breach is pleadable now; the dollars are the largest
explicit unknown in the case and the top item in the follow-on records demand.

Plead these as derivative counts with demand futility pleaded in the same
breath: the sole manager is the interested party on every one of them, which the
library says makes futility readily pleadable (§18-1001; *Zuckerberg*,
library/tooley_direct_derivative.md). Do not plead unjust enrichment or waste as
standalone counts — the express contract covers the same subject matter and
those theories are the first casualties of a §7.3 elimination.

## What I would file, where, and how funded

**Where:** Court of Chancery. §11.2 selects it and §11.1 picks Delaware law.
No forum choice to litigate.

**What:** one complaint, three counts. (1) Declaratory judgment — the
2026-08-20 call failed §6.3's certified-mail-and-email requirement and lacked a
stated bona fide purpose, so no Dilution Event occurred and Imani remains an 18%
Member. (2) Implied covenant — the $630,504-$768,908 implied valuation is an
arbitrary, bad-faith exercise of §6.3 discretion; void or recompute the
dilution. (3) Derivative breach of §5.4 as to both fee streams, with demand
futility pleaded and damages/disgorgement to the Company. Add the Dieckman
framing as the theory running through counts 1-2 — a defective notice and an
unexplained valuation engineered to strip a minority member of the fruits of the
bargain — rather than as a freestanding count.

**Skip:** any count attacking Book Value as a price. §9.2 says Book Value, book
value is what it is, and the implied covenant is a gap-filler that cannot
override express terms (*Nemec v. Shrader*,
library/implied_covenant_cases.md). If there is an appraisal right or a
fair-value floor hiding in the parts of the agreement we were not given, that
changes — the client does not have the complete LLC agreement and has no further
information (explicit unknown), which is the main reason the follow-on records
demand targets it before filing.

**How funded — the honest part.** **[CONFIRMED]** Imani cannot fund the
$270,000 capital call, and roughly **$60,000** is what she can put toward this
matter in total. Two consequences. First, do not send money toward the call:
§6.3 has no partial-funding mechanic, the funding window has closed, and a
tender would implicitly acknowledge the validity of a call the complaint will
say never triggered. The $60,000 is a litigation budget, not a down payment.
Second, $60,000 does not buy a Chancery case through trial — litigation against
a manager with a self-dealing record runs well into six figures, and Delaware
does not shift fees as a matter of course; bad-faith fee-shifting exists in
Chancery practice but **nothing in this library supports it, so I flag it as
unverified and will not build the plan on it.** The funding plan is therefore
sequencing, not heroics: a scoped first phase that $60,000 can actually pay for,
and a repricing event that attracts better funding for phase two.

1. **Targeted books-and-records follow-on demand.** The 2026 demand already got
   the bank statements; now aim at what is still missing: the complete LLC
   agreement, rent rolls 2022-2026, the 2024/2025 year-end balance sheets, the
   Castellan engagement file and invoices, the capital-account ledger, and the
   file on the $1.5M call (§18-1001 to 18-1003,
   library/tooley_direct_derivative.md; a §18-305-style demand is the cheap route
   to all of it. Note: §18-305 is **not in this library** — unverified, flagged,
   do not cite it). This is a
   weeks-long, low-cost first move that either supplies the rent roll (which
   could double the fee claim) or supplies a clean statutory record of the
   Company's refusal.
2. **Move for partial summary judgment on the notice defect**, not a full
   trial. The facts are on paper and mostly in the Manager's own produced
   minutes: text message, no certified mail, dead email, old address on the
   Company's file. One count, one issue, decided on documents — and scoped so
   that the complaint plus this one motion is what the $60,000 actually buys.
   That single win
   restores 18% and reprices the case from a $189,100 offer to a $1,764,000
   negotiating position.
3. **Then** decide whether to press the derivative fee counts, and with what
   budget — and consider at that point whether contingency or hybrid counsel, or
   a funder, will look at a case whose headline count has already been resolved
   in her favor. Nothing in the library addresses funding or fee recovery; that
   is a market question, not a legal one.
4. Keep the Company's own offer mechanism open as the exit. §9.2 says the
   Company "may purchase" — it is Bram's option, not hers. If the Company
   declines to buy at any price, Imani is an 18% Member of a Delaware LLC with no
   exit, no duties owed to her by the Manager, and a hostile 82% owner. That is
   the real downside of this case, and it is why I am not recommending "litigate
   to trial and never negotiate."

## The number to ask for, and the number to walk past

Ask for the deal that gives back the dilution and buys her out at the
Manager's own valuation: **18% × $7.2M = $1,296,000**. That number is
unassailable in a specific way — it is his own fair-value figure from
2026-09-25, so he cannot call it inflated, and it lets him settle without
adopting the broker's $9.8M. Anchor above it at the broker figure, **$1,764,000**,
with the fee claims (the full tolled $1,050,000 plus the unquantified 6% rent
stream) as the reason to move fast.

The floor is roughly **$597,800** — 6.1% × $9.8M — which is what her interest is
worth on her own best valuation evidence *even if the dilution stands*. Below
that she is better off litigating count 1, which is the strongest count in the
file. Any settlement must also release the derivative fee claims in the
Company's name, which needs the Company's signature and raises a procedural
point to think through before signing (**unverified** — derivative settlement
approval requirements are not in this library).

## Risks, stated plainly

- **The stale-address defence, now with two branches.** **[CONFIRMED]** the
  email went to an address on the Company's own file that has been dead since
  2019, and no certified letter was ever sent — the failure is entirely the
  Company's, which is good. But the confirmation also gave the defense two
  arguments: (1) *prejudice* — Imani **read the text** the day it was sent, so
  Bram will argue the missing letter harmed no one and Chancery in equity may
  care about harm even where *Zimmerman* strictly requires the specified
  channel; and (2) *acquiescence* — she let the 2026-09-20 deadline and the
  2026-09-25 dilution declaration pass without objecting because she "assumed
  it wasn't real notice." Nothing in the excerpts deems text delivery effective
  or requires prompt objection, but expect both arguments; they are why count 1
  is high, not certain. (No question to ask — the client's account is the
  record.)
- **The fee claim shrinks — but from a stronger base.** Tolling is now the
  primary, client-confirmed position (affiliate fees first surfaced in the 2026
  books-and-records production; before that only one-page "management
  expenses" summaries naming no payee), so the claim is pleaded at the full
  $1,050,000. The residual risk is that a court finds those summaries
  disclosed enough of the injury to start the clock, costing 2022 and 2023 =
  $375,000. The 6%-of-gross-rents stream stays unquantified until someone
  produces a rent roll — it is the largest unknown in the case, not a line
  item to assume. And damages on §5.4 are measured against an arm's-length
  price, not the full amount paid; the third-party fee benchmark is itself an
  unknown.
- **The broker opinion is one-sided and untested.** It is Imani's own evidence,
  dated three days before this engagement, and Bram will attack it. It is the
  ceiling of the claim, not the value.
- **Book equity of $3.1M against a $14.5M enterprise value is a large gap.**
  Some of it is ordinary depreciation on real estate; the broker spread implies
  about $4.7M of net debt the file never mentions. It is a fairness argument, not
  a legal defect in the price (§9.2 says Book Value, and it is enforceable).
- **We are working from excerpts of the operating agreement.** Any withdrawal,
  drag-along, appraisal, or deemed-default provision we have not seen could
  reprice everything. The client does not have the complete LLC agreement and
  has no further information to give, so the follow-on records demand (and,
  if refused, the complaint) is the only route to it — which makes getting the
  full agreement the highest-value single task before filing.
- **She may end up still in the company.** If Bram simply stops pursuing the
  buyout, count 1 gives her back an 18% interest in an LLC whose Manager owes
  her no duties, with a dilution mechanism that will be run again properly this
  time. That is a reason to negotiate off the strengthened position rather than
  to chase a judgment.
- **Costs.** **[CONFIRMED]** budget of roughly **$60,000**, a forum that does
  not routinely shift fees, and a defendant who can fund years of litigation.
  $60,000 buys the records demand, the complaint, and one narrowed summary-
  judgment motion — not a trial. Sequencing is the whole funding strategy, and
  the sequencing only works if the first motion is actually filed promptly;
  every plan past that point assumes the notice win has repriced the case for
  a contingency partner or a funder.
