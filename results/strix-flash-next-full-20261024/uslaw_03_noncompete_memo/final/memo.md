## 1. Question Presented

Meridian's employment agreement with Wei-Lin Zhao contains §8, a 24-month, nationwide non-compete covering "any business engaged in the design or sale of autonomous mobile robots for warehouse logistics," and clauses selecting Texas law (§14) and exclusive Travis County jurisdiction (§14). Zhao lived and worked in California for the entire ~31-month employment, signed the form agreement without an attorney, resigned effective September 30, 2026, and started at Kestrel Autonomy (Fremont, CA) on October 13, 2026. ## 2. Brief Answer

**No. Do not file in Travis County, and do not send any demand that asks Zhao to stop working at Kestrel.**

- **Chance §8 actually restrains Zhao (lasting injunctive relief in any forum that reaches him): ~5–10%.** Two independent gates each cut against Meridian: (1) Labor Code §925 makes the Texas forum and choice-of-law clauses voidable by Zhao on facts the file squarely establishes, and once voided, California law governs by statute; (2) even if a Texas court applied Texas law and reformed §8 (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.51(c)), an injunction would face non-recognition in California on public-policy grounds, which is where Zhao lives and works.
- **Chance §8 is held void if California law applies: ~90–95%.** Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600 voids employment non-competes "no matter how narrowly tailored"; no library authority supplies an applicable exception, and the "narrow restraint" escape routes (*Campbell*, *General Commercial Packaging*) were rejected in *Edwards*.
- **Chance Meridian pays Zhao's fees if it sues (or sends an enforcement demand): ~60–80%.** Two separate fee provisions are in play: Lab. Code §925(c) (court *may* award fees to an employee enforcing §925) and §16600.5(e)(2) (a prevailing employee on a §16600.5 claim *shall* recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs).
- **Meridian already has exposure, independent of any suit.** Under the library's statutory text, Meridian *entered into* a contract containing a void clause on March 4, 2024 — a violation of §16600.5(c) and a "civil violation" under §16600.5(d) — and §16600.1(a) makes including such a clause unlawful. Zhao's resignation email states he has consulted a California employment attorney, who advises him that §8 is void and that he may void the Texas clauses. The single worst available action is a §8 enforcement demand or filing, which would itself complete a §16600.5(b) violation and hand Zhao his fee claim.

Probability figures are our judgment on these file facts, not statistics. The client's Oct. 25, 2026 responses confirmed the key facts (Oakland-only workplace; no misappropriation evidence; LO-233 closed "won't do"); they firm the direction and we adjusted Scenario C modestly upward (§4.8). Remaining open items in §9 move figures modestly, not directionally.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Gate one: can Meridian even keep the case in Travis County? (§925)

Cal. Lab. Code §925 (in force; applies to contracts entered into on or after Jan. 1, 2017 per §925(f)) bars an employer from requiring an employee "who primarily resides and works in California, as a condition of employment," to agree to a clause that (1) requires adjudication outside California of "a claim arising in California," or (2) deprives the employee of the substantive protection of California law for a controversy arising in California. §925(a). Zhao lived and worked in Oakland for the entire employment; his claims (is §8 void? was it unlawfully enforced?) arise in California. Both §14 clauses do exactly what §925(a) prohibits.

The only exception, §925(e), is for an employee "in fact individually represented by legal counsel **in negotiating the terms**." The file is the opposite of that exception: standard form, "no negotiation; no attorney review requested or offered," signed 113 minutes after receipt, and §15's counsel acknowledgment is affirmatively contradicted by the HR file. Meridian drafted §15; it will not survive contact with the HR timeline, and it is itself a bad fact — a contractual recitation the company's own records dispute.

Consequence: §925(b) — the clauses are **voidable by the employee**, and if voided, "the matter shall be adjudicated in California and California law shall govern the dispute." Zhao's resignation email already reports his attorney's advice that he may do exactly this. Additionally, §925(c) permits a fee award to an employee enforcing §925 rights.

Two honest caveats:

- **Whether a Texas court honors §925 is not resolved by anything in the library.** A Travis County judge does not automatically apply California statutes. Zhao's practical move is to race to California — file first for declaratory relief — and his resignation email shows his attorney is already advising him toward exactly that (§925-style voidability announced on its face). If the case ends up in a California court, §925(b) resolves the forum and choice-of-law question by statute and §187 analysis below becomes belt-and-suspenders.
- **Personal jurisdiction if the clause is voided.** Our library's practice note (`tx_jurisdiction_note.md`, on Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §17.042 and *Michiana Easy Livin' Country v. Holten*, 168 S.W.3d 777 (Tex. 2005)) states the forum clause generally supplies consent to jurisdiction — but if §925 voids it at Zhao's election, Texas courts would need independent minimum contacts, and "[t]wo short business trips to Austin and a contract with a Texas employer may or may not suffice." The client has now **confirmed those two trips are Zhao's entire physical Texas footprint** — exactly the fact pattern the note says "may or may not suffice." A personal-jurisdiction fight could dispose of a Travis County suit before the merits.
- *Nedlloyd Lines B.V. v. Superior Court*, 3 Cal.4th 459 (1992), is the fair counterweight: California courts respect forum/choice-of-law clauses made at arm's length. That is precisely why §925(e)'s negotiated-with-counsel carve-out exists — and why it does not fit these facts. *Ixchel Pharma v. Biogen*, 9 Cal.5th 1130 (2020), confirms the more permissive side of California's doctrine applies only to **business-to-business** restraints (rule of reason) and expressly keeps *Edwards* — voidness — for the employment context; Zhao's covenant is pure employment, so *Ixchel* is Meridian's dead end, not its opening. *Advanced Bionics Corp. v. Medtronic*, 29 Cal.4th 697 (2002), adds that in non-compete disputes California courts generally will not enjoin parallel out-of-state litigation — so Meridian should not count on a California court simply blocking a Travis County case either; interstate racing and friction are the realistic posture.

### 4.2 Gate two: whose law governs enforceability? (Restatement §187; *DeSantis*; *Application Group*)

Both California and Texas apply Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §187: the parties' chosen law applies **unless** (a) the chosen state lacks a substantial relationship, or (b) the chosen law is **contrary to a fundamental policy** of a state with a **materially greater interest** that would supply the law absent the clause. *Nedlloyd* (California); *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990) (Texas); `restatement_187.md`. (Texas is also in play independently: §925(b) imposes California law if Zhao voids §14; and Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.52 makes the statutory covenant rules exclusive under Texas law.)

Prong (a) is not Meridian's route — Texas has a substantial relationship (Austin headquarters per the HR file; hiring and contract administration run out of Austin), so the argument stands or falls on prong (b):

- **Fundamental policy:** California's policy voiding employment non-competes is expressly "fundamental." *Application Group, Inc. v. Hunter Group, Inc.*, 61 Cal.App.4th 881 (1998). §16600's own text (as amended by SB 699) directs broad reading.
- **Materially greater interest:** *Application Group* is the closest analogue in the library — a Maryland employer's non-compete, enforced through its choice-of-law clause, did not control; California had the materially greater interest in applying §16600 to a non-compete with an employee recruited to work for a California employer. Zhao's residence, workplace, and new employment are all California. Meridian's Texas contacts are its Austin headquarters and Austin-administered hiring; Zhao's entire physical Texas footprint was two short business trips.
- **Decisive point for venue planning:** *DeSantis* is the Texas Supreme Court doing this **against an out-of-state choice-of-law clause in a non-compete** — it refused to apply a Florida law clause for an employee working in Texas because the state with the materially greater interest had a fundamental contrary policy. The same reasoning, mirrored, tells a Travis County judge that California law supplies the rule for a California worker. Meridian cannot assume a Texas forum buys Texas law; *DeSantis* is Zhao's weapon in Meridian's chosen courthouse.

If the §187 fight is lost (or §925(b) applies by statute), California law governs and §8 is void. If Meridian somehow wins the choice-of-law fight, it still needs Texas law to give it a remedy — see §4.3 — and then still needs California to recognize the judgment — it won't (`tx_jurisdiction_note.md`).

### 4.3 Enforceability under Texas law (the fallback, analyzed honestly)

Under Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.50(a) (in force), a covenant is enforceable only if **ancillary to or part of an otherwise enforceable agreement** when made, and only to the extent its limits on **time, geography, and scope of activity** are reasonable and no greater than necessary to protect the promisee's business interest. §15.51(b) puts the burden on Meridian because the agreement's primary purpose is personal services. §15.52 makes these statutory criteria exclusive.

- **Ancillarity:** probably satisfiable. *Sheshunoff v. Johnson*, 209 S.W.3d 644 (Tex. 2006), held a covenant in an at-will agreement is ancillary when the employer's return promise (e.g., providing confidential information) is performed — and it modified *Light v. Centel Cellular*, 883 S.W.2d 642 (Tex. 1994), which we cite **only for its status**: *Light*'s same-time consideration requirement is no longer law. So the fact that Zhao signed before receiving confidential information is not fatal under current Texas law. *Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011), holds stock options can supply the needed consideration link — but nothing in the produced file evidences equity or option terms in Zhao's contract, so that *Marsh* path exists only if the unproduced remainder of the agreement contains them (flagged in `QUESTIONS.md`, not assumed).
- **Reasonableness:** §8 is the problem. 24 months, nationwide, every AMR warehouse-logistics business. *Sheshunoff* enforced an ancillary covenant in an at-will agreement, with reasonableness under §15.50 as the central inquiry; *Marsh* involved a covenant tied to goodwill via stock options — neither blesses a two-year nationwide bar on an engineer's core occupation. The statute's own standard — limitations as to time, geography, and scope that are "reasonable and do not impose a greater restraint than is necessary" to protect the business interest (§15.50(a)) — is where §8 fails. (The Act file in our library covers §15.50(a), §15.51(b)–(c), §15.52 only; any further Texas limiting provisions, such as rules on restraints covering general skills, are outside the library and are not relied on here.)
- **The Texas answer is reformation, not invalidity:** §15.51(c) — if limitations are unreasonable, the court **shall reform** and enforce the reformed covenant, but **may not award damages for breach before reformation**. So even Meridian's best Texas-law day produces a reformed covenant (narrower time/geography/scope) and zero damages for Zhao's first months at Kestrel. Note also that the excerpt of the Act in our library covers §§15.50(a), 15.51(b)–(c), 15.52; the Texas statutory basis for injunctive relief is not in the library, so we flag rather than brief it.
- **And the reformed injunction dies at the state line.** Our library's Texas practice note states the conclusion directly: an injunction entered in Texas "would in any event face non-recognition in California on public-policy grounds for the non-compete." Zhao lives and works in California. A Texas decree would be a paper victory Meridian pays to obtain and cannot enforce.

### 4.4 Enforceability under California law

§16600 (as amended by SB 699, eff. Jan. 1, 2024): "every contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void," to be "read broadly, in accordance with *Edwards*... to void the application of any noncompete agreement in an employment context... **no matter how narrowly tailored**, that does not satisfy an exception in this chapter."

- *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP*, 44 Cal.4th 937 (2008): non-competes are void in the employment context unless a **statutory** exception applies; the Ninth Circuit's "narrow restraint" gloss was rejected, and *Campbell v. Board of Trustees*, 817 F.2d 499 (9th Cir. 1987), and *General Commercial Packaging*, 126 F.3d 1131 (9th Cir. 1997), were disapproved. We cite *Campbell* and *General Commercial Packaging* **only for their superseded status**; a narrow-restraint defense for §8 is not available. (Worth noting: even under the old narrow-restraint view, a 24-month nationwide bar on Zhao's core occupation is not a restraint on only a "small or limited part of the business, trade or profession.")
- The statutory exceptions are in "this chapter"; the library contains §16600, §16600.1, and §16600.5 but **not** the sale-of-business/partnership exceptions (§§16601 et seq.), and nothing in the file suggests Zhao sold a business or partnership interest — equity compensation is not an exception the library supports. If Meridian wants an exception argument, it must come from authorities outside this library, which we cannot verify here — we note it as **unverified and unsupported on this file**, not as a defense.
- *Ixchel Pharma v. Biogen* (rule of reason) does not help: it applies the rule of reason to **business-to-business** restraints and expressly keeps *Edwards* governing the employment context.
- **No federal hook either:** the FTC Non-Compete Clause Rule, 16 C.F.R. pt. 910, was set aside nationwide in *Ryan LLC v. FTC*, 739 F. Supp. 3d 496 (N.D. Tex. 2024) — the FTC voluntarily dismissed its appeal in September 2025, and the Rule never took effect. It provides Meridian nothing, and Zhao nothing new; state law is the whole game. (We flag the Rule and *Ryan* only to close the door.)

Under California law, §8 is void. Our estimate of ~90–95% reflects that the only real questions are choice-of-law/venue gates, not the merits.

### 4.5 Cal. Lab. Code §925 (detail in §4.1) — the forum/choice-of-law clauses are voidable

Summary: §925(a)–(c) + (e)–(f); no counsel, no negotiation, form contract, California residence and workplace; §15 acknowledgment contradicted by HR's own timeline. Zhao elects voidability → California forum, California law, potential §925(c) fee award. This is the most probable end of the case, and it happens before anyone briefs §15.50.

### 4.6 §16600.5 exposure — Meridian's own violations

§16600.5 (SB 699, eff. Jan. 1, 2024), per the library text:

- **(a)** Any contract void under the chapter is unenforceable "regardless of where and when the contract was signed."
- **(b)** An employer "shall not **attempt to enforce**" a void contract "regardless of whether the contract was signed and the employment was maintained outside of California."
- **(c)** An employer "shall not enter into a contract with an employee or prospective employee that includes a provision that is void under this chapter."
- **(d)** An employer that **enters into** such a contract **or attempts to enforce** one "commits a civil violation."
- **(e)** The employee may sue for **injunctive relief or actual damages**; a **prevailing employee shall recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs**.

Applied to these facts:

1. **A violation has already happened — signing.** Meridian "entered into" an agreement including a §16600-void clause on March 4, 2024, 63 days after §16600.5 took effect. On the plain text, §16600.5(c) was violated at signing and (d) makes it a civil violation. Zhao's remedy set is currently thin — he has a job, no documented lost income, so actual damages look small — but the claim exists and the fee-shifting in (e)(2) makes suing for it attractive to his counsel.
2. **A second violation is entirely Meridian's to avoid: the "attempt to enforce."** Filing the Travis County suit, or a cease-and-desist letter invoking §8, is the clearest possible "attempt to enforce." That converts a stale signing violation into a live, fee-generating claim. **No demand letter has been sent yet. Keep it that way.**
3. **The extraterritoriality defense is not reliable.** §16600.5(a)–(b) speak in terms deliberately indifferent to where the contract was signed or the employment maintained. The firm's 2024 memo leaned on *Westbrook Systems v. Ng* for the proposition that §16600.5 does not apply to out-of-state employers — but (i) *Westbrook* and *Calderon v. Pacific Dynamics* are cited **inside the firm memos only; they are not in the library and cannot be verified here**, and (ii) the firm's own 2025 update records that the Ninth Circuit **granted rehearing en banc in *Westbrook*** (argued Jan. 2025), so the cited panel decision was under review and should not be treated as settled. Moreover, *Westbrook*'s premise — employment maintained outside California — does not even fit: Zhao's employment was maintained in California. Until verified authority says otherwise, plan on §16600.5 applying.

### 4.7 The AB 1076 notice problem (§16600.1) — and company-wide exposure

§16600.1 (AB 1076, eff. Jan. 1, 2024): (a) it is **unlawful to include** a non-compete in an employment contract (or to require an employee to enter one) that does not satisfy a statutory exception; (b) for **current employees**, and **former employees employed after Jan. 1, 2022**, whose contracts include such a clause, the employer **shall notify the employee by Feb. 14, 2024**, in a written individualized communication delivered to last known address **and** email, that the clause is void; (c) a violation "constitutes an act of unfair competition within the meaning of Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 17200)."

Two findings for Meridian:

- **As to Zhao:** §16600.1(a) was violated when §8 went into his March 4, 2024 contract — 63 days after (a) took effect. The (b) *notice* duty is genuinely ambiguous for him: the deadline (Feb. 14, 2024) came **14 days before Meridian even made the offer** and 19 days before he was hired. One reading says a hire after the deadline never triggers the fixed-date duty; the other says he became a "current employee" whose contract contained a void clause and notice was owed within a reasonable time. The library contains only the statutory text; no case resolving this is in the library, so we flag it as **unverified on the case law**. Either way, the absence of notice changes nothing about §8's voidness — but see the next point.
- **As to everyone else — the bigger problem:** Legal ops ticket LO-233 records that the AB 1076 notice project was "deprioritized" and **no notice was sent to California employees, company-wide**. For every current California employee and post-2022 former California employee with a §8-style clause who was under contract by the Feb. 14, 2024 deadline, the plain text of (b) was violated when that deadline passed, and each violation is a UCL act under (c). Post-deadline California hires — Zhao himself among them — sit in the ambiguity flagged in the previous bullet. This exposure is independent of Zhao and probably larger than Zhao. The number of affected people is **unknown — the client has no additional information** (§9, `QUESTIONS.md`). (The UCL remedies themselves are outside the library — §§17200 et seq. are not in it — so we flag rather than quantify them.)
- **The LO-233 entry is the worst document in the file** on the knowledge question, and it has gotten worse. As of February 2024, Legal had an AB 1076 notice project on the books; the record shows the project "deprioritized," **no** notice sent to California employees, and the ticket ultimately closed as **"won't do"** (client-confirmed Oct. 25, 2026). That is a documented, deliberate decision not to comply — not an oversight — and on March 4, 2024, after the February 14 statutory deadline had passed, Meridian put a new void non-compete into a new California hire's contract. If Zhao ever litigates willfulness-adjacent theories, that is the exhibit. The affected population (current employees plus former employees after Jan. 1, 2022, with §8-style clauses working in California) is **unknown — the client has no additional information** — so we cannot quantify this exposure and we do not force it to a number.

## 5. Strongest Argument for Each Side

**Meridian's best:** The choice-of-law/forum clauses are presumptively valid — Texas has a substantial relationship (Austin HQ, Austin-administered hiring), and under *Nedlloyd* arm's-length clauses command deference; §15 recites Zhao had the opportunity to consult counsel. If a Texas court applies Texas law, §15.50(a)/§15.51(c) guarantees *some* enforceable covenant — *Sheshunoff* establishes that a performed confidential-information promise supports an ancillary covenant, and *Marsh* would do the same for equity terms **if** the unproduced remainder of the agreement contains any (the excerpt shows none) — and Meridian at least gets reformation rather than total invalidity. **Candidly: this argument's load-bearing pieces are the §15 recital (contradicted by the HR file) and the assumption that Texas law applies (contradicted by *DeSantis*). It is the weakest form of the case, and it still ends at the California border.**

**Zhao's best:** §925. The statute does the work on the employer's own documents: California residence and workplace, form contract, 113-minute signing, no counsel, no negotiation, and a contractual recital the company's HR timeline refutes. Once §14 is voidable and voided, §925(b) supplies the California forum and California law by statute; §16600 then voids §8 without any balancing; and §16600.5(c)–(d) means Meridian was already in violation the day it hired him. He has already announced it, in the resignation email: a California employment attorney "advises me that §8 is void under California law and that I may void the Texas forum and choice-of-law clauses." And the client's own confirmation — no evidence he took anything; "the competing itself" is the objection — concedes the case is about a pure restraint of trade, which is precisely what §16600 voids.

## 6. Recommendation

1. **Do not file in Travis County.** Do not issue any demand letter, notice, or communication that invokes §8 to restrain Zhao's Kestrel employment. (This also means: no "preservation" letters to Kestrel that demand Zhao stop working — they are attempts to enforce.)
2. **Send a §16600.1(b)-conforming notice to Zhao** — individualized written notice, by mail to last known address and email, stating §8 is void and Meridian will not seek to enforce it. Have counsel send it, not the line manager.
3. **Inventory the company-wide AB 1076 failure now.** Identify all current employees and former employees (employed after Jan. 1, 2022) with §8-style clauses who primarily work in California, and send conforming notices. The affected population is currently unknown to the client — the inventory itself is step one. LO-233 was logged "deprioritized" on the Feb. 14, 2024 deadline date itself, and was ultimately closed "won't do" (closing date not shown in the file); the UCL exposure under §16600.1(c) compounds with every employee left un-noticed. This is a bigger liability than Zhao and it is fixable.
4. **Fix the form.** The same §8 went into a California hire's contract 19 days after the AB 1076 notice deadline passed. Every new California-based hire signing this form is a fresh §16600.5(c) violation. Remove §8 for California-based hires (and check whether Meridian has used the form elsewhere; that is beyond this library's scope).
5. **Do not build a §10 case that does not exist.** The client confirms there is no evidence Zhao accessed, took, or retained anything, and that the objection is to the competing itself — which §8 cannot lawfully reach and §10 does not either. Zhao affirmatively states he will honor confidentiality. §10 (confidentiality/inventions) and §9 (12-month non-solicit) are separate covenants whose California-law treatment is not briefed in this library; on this record there is no trade-secret case to bring, and §8 must not be enforced as a proxy for §10. If actual misuse evidence ever emerges, that is a different case requiring authorities beyond this file.
6. **Reconsider reliance on the 2024/2025 firm memos** (the memo-bank files in the library). Their core support — *Calderon* and *Westbrook* — is unverifiable on this library, and the 2025 update itself notes rehearing en banc was granted in *Westbrook*. ## 10. Addendum — GC Questions (Dana, Oct. 25, 2026): *Calderon*/*Westbrook* and the FTC Rule

**Q1: "Our outside counsel's 2024 memo says *Calderon* and *Westbrook* support exactly this. Why aren't you relying on them?"**

Three reasons; each is independently sufficient not to rely on them.

1. **They are unverifiable.** Both cases appear *only inside the firm's own memos* (`memo_bank/`); they are not in the library, and no full text exists anywhere in this file (the client confirmed no additional information). An engagement standard this office has applied to every authority — verify status or label unverified — applies equally to the firm's own prior advice. Cases we cannot read cannot be load-bearing for a filing decision.
2. **The firm itself flagged them as unsettled.** The 2025 update records that the Ninth Circuit **granted rehearing en banc in *Westbrook*** (argued Jan. 2025) and describes the position as maintained "pending further guidance." As of today there is no record of the en banc outcome. Betting a Travis County filing — and the §16600.5(b) exposure that filing creates — on an appellate status nobody in the file can confirm is not advice; it is a wager.
3. **Even taken exactly as the 2024 memo characterizes them, they do not fit these facts:**
   - *Westbrook* is described as holding "§16600.5 does not apply extraterritorially to contracts with out-of-state employers" — a theory whose premise is employment maintained **outside** California. The client has confirmed Zhao's employment was maintained **entirely in California** (Oakland the whole time; Austin = two trips). Whatever *Westbrook* holds, this is not its case. And the library's statutory text points the other way: §16600.5(b) reaches attempts to enforce "**regardless of whether the contract was signed and the employment was maintained outside of California**."
   - *Calderon* is described as enforcing a Texas choice-of-law clause against a remote California employee who "availed himself" of Texas benefits. Zhao's entire Texas "benefit" record is two short trips and a form contract signed in 113 minutes with no counsel and no negotiation. More fundamentally, **§925 is a statute** that makes exactly such clauses voidable for employees who primarily reside and work in California, with a carve-out only for employees "in fact individually represented by legal counsel in negotiating the terms" — the file shows none. As characterized, *Calderon* never engages §925 at all; a case we cannot read does not override a statute we can.
   - Neither case — as characterized — addresses the other two gates at all: §925's forum-voidability and the §187/*DeSantis* analysis. *DeSantis* is **Texas Supreme Court precedent binding in Travis County**, and it does precisely what Dana wants undone: it refuses to apply a contrary out-of-state law clause in a non-compete for the state with the materially greater interest and a fundamental contrary policy. Even a true believer in the 2024 memo's two cases must still win every gate.

**Q2: "The FTC rule is dead, so what's the problem?"**

The FTC Non-Compete Clause Rule would have **banned** non-competes — it was a sword against employers like Meridian, never a shield. Its set-aside (*Ryan LLC v. FTC*, N.D. Tex. 2024; appeal dismissed Sept. 2025, per the library) takes nothing away from Meridian because **Zhao's case never needed it**. Zhao's case rests on California statutes that long predate the FTC rule and are untouched by its death: §16600 (employment non-competes void, "no matter how narrowly tailored"), §16600.1 (unlawful to include; notice duty; UCL treatment), §16600.5 (no attempting to enforce; private action; prevailing employee recovers fees), and Lab. Code §925 (voidable forum/choice-of-law clauses). The firm's own 2025 update concedes the frame: "With the FTC rule enjoined, **state law controls**." The state law that controls here is California's — and that is the whole problem, FTC rule or no FTC rule.
