## 1. Schedule 3.3 — "we understand there are no consent issues"

That understanding is incorrect. The data room shows two contract consents and one governmental approval:

**(a) Hensley Homes Master Supply Agreement (data room §1.1; largest customer, 38% of 2025 revenue — 0.38 × $4.6M ≈ $1,748,000).** MSA §18 bars assignment "by operation of law, merger, sale of assets, change of control or otherwise" without prior written consent, "which may be withheld in its sole discretion," and provides that any purported assignment in violation is **void**. A clause in that form is enforced according to its terms; in an asset sale, transferring the contract without consent is a breach and the transfer fails. *SQL Solutions v. Oracle*, 1991 WL 626458 (N.D. Cal.); Restatement (Second) of Contracts §317; anti_assignment_change_of_control.md. Buyer presumably intends to acquire this contract — §3.1 covers the Assigned Contracts — so Hensley's written consent belongs on Schedule 3.3 and, in our view, in the closing conditions. Because consent is in Hensley's sole discretion, we flag now that Hensley may seek commercial concessions; who bears that cost is a deal term, not something a blank schedule resolves. On sequencing, Seller will lead the first Hensley conversation personally before consent paperwork circulates; if written consent is not forthcoming, the structured alternatives are carving the MSA out of the Assigned Contracts with a post-closing supply arrangement, or a consent-keyed price adjustment — not closing blind on a "None" schedule.

**(b) Shop lease, 7700 Burleson Rd (data room §3.1, lease §22).** Landlord consent required, not to be unreasonably withheld. Belongs on Schedule 3.3.

**(c) Cascade Builders (data room §1.2; 11% of revenue).** No consent required — assignment is permitted to a successor to substantially all assets upon notice. We will give written notice; no closing condition needed.

**(d) 22 residential contracts (data room §1.3; each <$40k).** Silent on assignment. Absent anti-assignment language, assignment of contract rights is generally permitted, and delegation of duties requires consent only where performance is personal. Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§317, 322; anti_assignment_change_of_control.md. No consents expected; we ask your client to accept a confirmation that no engagement is personal-services dependent (we are verifying on our side).

**(e) City of Austin spray-booth permit (data room §4.1).** Not an assignment issue — the permit is non-transferable and Buyer must reapply (30–60 days). This should be handled as a governmental approval in Schedule 3.3/closing mechanics, with the application filed pre-closing; it is on the critical path against your two-week signing target.

Proposed response: we will circulate a populated Schedule 3.3 (items (a), (b), (e), with (c) as a notice item). A "commercially reasonable efforts" covenant pointed at a schedule reading "None" protects no one and does not change what your client actually acquires.

## 2. WARN — "can you confirm headcount is under threshold?"

Confirmed on the data room census (data room §§2.1–2.2): **14 W-2 employees** (7 shop + 4 installers + 3 office). WARN applies to employers of **100 or more employees** (excluding part-time) and to plant closings or mass layoffs affecting 50+ employees at a single site; Texas has no mini-WARN statute. 29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(1); 20 C.F.R. §639.3; warn_act.md. Brightline is not a covered employer, so no WARN notice is required — including if your client does not hire every employee.

One caveat stated for the record: this conclusion does not depend on the worker-classification debate. Even counting the nine 1099 installers as employees (14 + 9 = 23), the business remains far below the 100-employee threshold. So your client's plan to issue no WARN notices is fine at this size — but it is fine because of headcount, not because classification is settled (see §3 and our redlines).

## 3. Our position on §7.4(c) (sandbagging)

We do not accept §7.4(c) as drafted, and we do not agree it is a neutral "simplification to market-standard knowledge language."

As drafted it is a **pro-sandbagging** clause: Buyer keeps full indemnity rights "regardless of knowledge acquired (or capable of being acquired) at any time, whether before or after execution." That includes everything in our data room. Under Delaware and Texas practice, a seller who discloses a known issue in diligence and then gives a clean representation subject to a pro-sandbagging clause has effectively agreed to indemnify for the disclosed issue; the recognized seller protections are an anti-sandbagging clause, a disclosure-schedule exception, or a special indemnity with a negotiated cap. sandbagging.md (*Akorn v. Fresenius* (Del. Ch. 2018) (dicta); ABA Private Target Deal Points Study). Market practice is split, not uniformly pro-sandbagging.

The problem is concrete, not theoretical. Your draft pairs §7.4(c) with (i) a clean §4.12 classification rep, (ii) a §7.4(b) carve-out putting §4.12 at a **full-purchase-price cap** (≈$3.1M on the implied price), and (iii) §7.4(d) survival to the statute of limitations. Your client's data room (data room §2.2: nine installers, exclusive, company tools/trucks, foreman-scheduled, hourly, $612,000 of 1099 payments in 2025) already shows these workers are employees under the common-law control test (IRS Rev. Rul. 87-41; worker_classification_tx_irs.md). The combined effect is that Ray indemnifies Lone Star — up to the entire price, personally, for 6+ years — for a problem Lone Star already knows about, with no dollar-matched security. A specific operational rep carved out to a full-price cap is also off-market; the market answer is a sized special indemnity. baskets_caps.md.

**Our proposal:**
1. §4.12 becomes "except as set forth on Schedule 4.12," with the installer program disclosed on that schedule, including that the installers work 45–50 hours per week in busy season and are paid straight hourly;
2. §7.4(c) becomes anti-sandbagging (no recovery for inaccuracy of a representation Buyer actually knew pre-closing, including from the data room and schedules) — fallback: pro-sandbagging except for matters disclosed in the data room or schedules;
3. §4.12 comes back under the general 10% cap; worker classification moves to a **special indemnity sized to the exposure** — we are prepared to discuss a cap in the **$125k–$200k** range, 30-month survival, secured within the existing escrow — instead of the full-price carve-out. That band is computed, not arbitrary: federal employment tax on the $612,000 of 2025 payments is roughly $56,800 over a 3-year lookback at the reduced non-willful §3509(a) rates; the FLSA straight-time shortfall for 9 hourly installers at $34–$41/hr working 45–50 hrs/wk in busy season is $765–$1,845 per busy week (0.5 × rate × OT hours × 9), i.e. $27,500–$143,900 over three years depending on busy-season length; combined ≈ **$84,300–$200,700**, before interest, penalties, liquidated damages, state UI, benefits and workers'-comp claims. (The tax rates and the half-time shortfall method are standard figures we state as working assumptions, not library citations; we will show the arithmetic on request.) A full-purchase-price cap of ~$3.1M on this rep is 15–35x the quantified band — baskets_caps.md calls a full-price carve-out for an operational rep off-market.

If Lone Star wants certainty on the classification issue, that is a price/special-indemnity conversation we can have openly — it should not be papered as a clean rep plus a knowledge-blind indemnity.

We also note §2.4 (EBITDA "as determined by Buyer") and §8.2 (7 years/500 miles) remain open; see our redline for proposed language on each (earnout: *Lazard Technology Partners v. Qinetiq*, 114 A.3d 193 (Del. 2015); non-compete: Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.50; *Marsh USA v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011)).
