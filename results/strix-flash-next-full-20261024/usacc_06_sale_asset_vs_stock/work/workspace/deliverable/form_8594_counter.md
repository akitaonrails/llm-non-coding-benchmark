## 2. Justification, class by class

**Class III — $200,000 (unchanged).** Face value of receivables per `documents/tax_history_and_conversion.md` ($200,000, no reserve) equals the amount allocated, which is the natural FMV of collected-in-the-ordinary-course trade receivables. Buyer's number is accepted. (Whether *you* recognize gain depends on the company's accounting method — see note 5.)

**Class IV — $150,000 (unchanged).** Books carry inventory at $140,000. Buyer's $150,000 is consistent with FMV of in-process and finished cabinetry materials, and inventory gain is ordinary at 37% in **any** allocation, so contesting it buys nothing. Concede it as a goodwill marker in the negotiation and bank the concession against Class V.

**Class V — $650,000, down from $900,000.** §1060 stops each class at fair market value before the residual moves on. The seller's contemporaneous fixed-asset register totals **$650,000** of FMV, and the 1/1/2023 conversion appraisal put equipment & vehicles at **$610,000**. There is no document in this file supporting $900,000. Buyer's over-allocation moves $250,000 from goodwill (20%) into property whose §1245 recapture is already total (depreciation allowed of **$860,000** exceeds any plausible gain), converting it into income taxed at **37%** — it costs you **$42,500** and is worth **nothing** to the buyer, who amortizes Class V and Class VII in essentially the same way. If the buyer believes the equipment is worth $900,000, require them to produce an appraisal; if they will not, FMV is $650,000.

**Class VI — $100,000, down from $400,000.** A covenant not to compete is a §197(d)(1)(E) intangible amortizable by the buyer over 15 years, and "**to the seller, payments for a covenant not to compete are ordinary income … not capital gain**" (`library/irc_197_and_noncompete.md`: Rev. Rul. 69-643; *Muskat v. United States*, 554 F.3d 183 (1st Cir. 2009)). The same library entry requires that the split between covenant and goodwill "**reflect economic reality**." Here the economic reality is documented in the competing offer: **Hensley requires a customary 3-year non-compete "as part of the purchase agreement at no separate consideration."** A covenant that one qualified buyer will take for nothing is not worth $400,000 to another. $100,000 is defensible as real consideration for a *five*-year restriction plus the consulting obligation in Buyer A ¶4 — enough that the covenant is not a nullity, low enough to survive scrutiny. The $300,000 difference belongs in Class VII and saves **$51,000**.

**Class VII — $2,000,000 (residual).** Under the residual method the balance of the consideration after Classes I–VI falls to goodwill and going concern value: 3,100,000 − 200,000 − 150,000 − 650,000 − 100,000 = **$2,000,000**. This is consistent with the buyer paying for a running cabinetry business (customer relationships, backlog, trained workforce, trade name), and it sits comfortably next to the $900,000 goodwill/trade-name appraisal at 1/1/2023 plus three years of growth. Basis in goodwill is $0, so the whole amount is gain; it is **long-term capital gain** at your level, of which **$900,000 is IRC §1374 recognized built-in gain** taxed at 21% inside the corporation (`library/irc_1374.md`, (d)(7)).

## 4. Notes and cautions on this schedule

1. **Get it signed.** Buyer A ¶3 says the allocation will "be reported consistently by both parties on IRS Form 8594." Signing Exhibit A locks you into *their* character split. Do not initial the LOI and plan to fix Form 8594 later.
2. **Do not let this become a bidding war on Class VI alone.** If Lone Star insists on a genuine 5-year covenant, the answer is to pay **more total consideration** for it (a real §197 intangible they amortize), not to relabel $400,000 of a fixed price.
3. **The residual must remain the largest item.** If Lone Star pushes Class V back above $650,000, every extra dollar costs you 17 cents; price the concession explicitly (they owe you $0.17 per dollar of over-allocation).
4. **[Unverified]** If any Class V item is real property or a structural improvement (the spray booth is the candidate), §1250/unrecaptured-gain treatment may apply and **is not in the reference library**; get an appraisal and confirm before filing. Also confirm whether the shop is leased or owned — if Brightline owns land or a building, it belongs in Class V and is missing from both Exhibit A and this schedule.
5. **[Open item]** If Brightline reports on the cash method, its receivables have zero basis and Class III produces **$200,000 of ordinary income** (+$74,000 tax). Buyer A should be told this before agreeing to buy receivables at face; alternatively, have the company collect the receivables before closing and exclude them from the sale.
6. This schedule does **not** apply to Buyer B's offer, which is a purchase of membership interests — no Form 8594, no allocation, and no §1245/§1374 exposure at the seller's level. If Hensley asks for a **§338(h)(10) or §336(e)** election, this schedule (and this whole tax problem) would be imported into the stock deal; refuse without a price increase that pays for the difference (`library/stock_vs_asset_overview.md`).
