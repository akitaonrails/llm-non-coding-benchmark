## 1. Mesa Steel February invoice — $62,400, dated 2/2/2026 (pre-petition)

**Why it cannot be paid.**

1. **Dkt. 61 ¶2 (Final DIP Order):** DIP proceeds "shall be used solely for post-petition operating
   expenses, administrative expenses allowed by the Court, and the Carve-Out. **Nothing herein authorizes
   payment of any pre-petition claim except as separately ordered by this Court.**" The February invoice is
   pre-petition (invoice date 2/2/2026 < petition date 3/10/2026).
2. **It is not "separately ordered."** The critical-vendor order, Dkt. 48 ¶1 and Schedule 1, lists **only
   Apex Coatings, Inc. ($31,200)** and states "**No other vendor is included.** Any motion to add a vendor
   requires notice and a hearing." Mesa is not on Schedule 1.
3. **Cap headroom is only $3,800.** Dkt. 48 ¶1 caps pre-petition critical-vendor payments at **$35,000
   aggregate**; Apex already consumed $31,200 (paid 3/24). $35,000 − $31,200 = **$3,800**. Even an amended
   order could not reach $62,400 without also increasing the aggregate cap.
4. **Code consequence:** payment of a pre-petition unsecured claim outside the ordinary course and without a
   court order is an **unauthorized post-petition transfer** the estate may avoid and recover from the
   recipient — 11 U.S.C. §549(a) (`library/bc_362_363_503_549.md`). Mesa's goods were delivered before the
   petition, so they are **not** §503(b)(1) administrative expenses (same file).
5. **Budget:** Dkt. 61 ¶1 allows only 10% variance per line item on a cumulative four-week basis against the
   Approved Budget (Exhibit B). A $62,400 unbudgeted disbursement risks a budget default; Exhibit B was not
   given to me (Q5).

**What it would cost.** Per the plan term sheet, general unsecured claims receive **35%** cash over 24 months.
Paying Mesa in full now costs **$62,400**; plan treatment costs **$21,840** (62,400 × 35%) — a **$40,560**
premium, plus it prefers one trade creditor ahead of the rest of the pool and invites objection.

**What I can lawfully do to get the steel.**

  (`library/bc_362_363_503_549.md`); DIP proceeds may fund them (Dkt. 61 ¶2). This one is **unpaid at 3/31** and
  should be paid now. (Do not let Mesa condition it on the February claim — see item 2.)
- **Move to add Mesa to Dkt. 48 Schedule 1** (notice and hearing required), with the written "Customary Trade
  Terms" agreement the order conditions payment on, and an increased aggregate cap. Until that order enters,
  the February claim stays in the GUC pool.

  on a pre-petition claim. The library index lists 11 U.S.C. §362(a) (`library/bc_362_363_503_549.md`) but the

## 7. Supplement — your follow-up: "Mesa owes us about $11,000 for coils we returned in January. I figured we'd just net it."

**No — netting is the same violation in a different costume, and it is the one thing Dkt. 61 names explicitly.**

### What netting actually does

Your receivable from Mesa (~$11,000 for the January returned coils) is **property of the estate** — a pre-petition
receivable the estate can collect in cash. Netting it against Mesa's February pre-petition invoice does three things
at once:

1. **It pays a pre-petition claim in kind, without a court order.** Applying an $11,000 estate asset to Mesa's
   $62,400 pre-petition invoice is a payment on that claim — $11,000 ÷ $62,400 = **17.63%** of the claim settled
   outside the case. The variant where you pay $51,400 cash from the DIP account instead of $62,400 is the same
   arithmetic ($62,400 − $11,000 = $51,400). Neither is authorized: Mesa is not on Dkt. 48 Schedule 1 ("No other
   vendor is included").
2. **It violates Dkt. 61 ¶3 directly.** That paragraph prohibits "setoff, recoupment **or netting**" against
   pre-petition obligations — netting is the prohibited method by name. It also cuts against ¶2 (DIP proceeds only
   for post-petition operating expenses, court-ordered administrative items, and the Carve-Out).
3. **It is an avoidable post-petition transfer.** Under 11 U.S.C. §549(a) (library/bc_362_363_503_549.md) a transfer
   of estate property made post-petition without court authorization can be avoided — here, transferred **to Mesa**,
   meaning the estate would have to claw its own $11,000 back from the vendor you are trying to keep.

There is no estate benefit to trade against that risk: you surrender an asset collectible in cash to shrink a claim
the plan pays at 35% anyway (plan term sheet), and you misstate the MOR — Mesa's Part 2 claim would be understated
by $11,000 and an $11,000 asset would disappear from the books.

### How the books treat it

- Mesa's pre-petition claim stays in **liabilities subject to compromise at gross $62,400** (Part 2 total stays
  **$74,110** — unchanged).
- The ~$11,000 coil credit is carried as a **separate estate asset** (pre-petition receivable from Mesa) and pursued
  for collection. Do not net it on the ledger against either the pre-petition payable or the post-petition payable.
  The library's reorganization-presentation rule (`library/asc852_fresh_start.md`: liabilities subject to compromise
  reported separately; pre-petition liabilities are not netted against post-petition assets) points the same way;
  note that here both items are pre-petition, so the controlling prohibitions are Dkt. 61 ¶3 and §549(a), not the
  accounting rule. Whether a true legal right of setoff even exists (11 U.S.C. §§506/558) cannot be analyzed from
  this file — those sections are **not in the library** [unverified].
- Get the credit memo / return documentation. "About $11,000" is an estimate; anything assertable to Mesa needs a
  supported number.
- **Assumption:** no netting has actually been booked or paid as of this writing. If it has, it must be reported in
  MOR Part 7 with no docket authority and unwound (§549(a) avoidance).

### The lawful way to get the economic result you want

If the goal is to recover the $11,000 cheaply from Mesa, the route is:

1. Keep billing and paying Mesa's **post-petition** invoices normally (the $18,900 March invoice is owed now —
   §§503(b)(1), 363(c)(1)).
2. Have counsel include, in the **Mesa critical-vendor motion** (add Mesa to Dkt. 48 and raise the $35,000 cap —
   headroom is only $3,800 after Apex's $31,200), a proposed **application of the documented ~$11,000 coil credit
   against Mesa's post-petition invoices**, as a compromise of the estate's receivable. Notice and hearing cure the
   §549/§363(b) problem, and Dkt. 61 ¶3 is not implicated because the payable being reduced is post-petition, not a
   pre-petition obligation. Whether an $11,000 ordinary-course trade compromise could instead be done without a
   motion under §363(c)(1) is counsel's judgment call — the library excerpt does not resolve it [unverified].

Do not book the netting before that approval.
