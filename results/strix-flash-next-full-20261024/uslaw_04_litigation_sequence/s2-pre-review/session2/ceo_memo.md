# Memo to CEO — the interference claim, plainly

**To:** Dolapo A. (CEO, Juniper Health Staffing) · **From:** Outside counsel · **Oct. 30, 2026**
**Privileged & confidential**

You asked: *"So we fold because of one email from Marcus? Hanna poached SEVEN people. Keep the
interference claim — I want them scared."* Three separate answers.

## 1. We did not fold. We kept 5 of 7 fees and traded $32,000 to protect everything else

Marcus's February 11 email waives fees for Rosa and Tay — $32,000. That is **7% of our $430,550.50
demand**. We conceded exactly that and nothing more: five conversion fees ($80,000 floor), all five
invoices ($336,900), and interest ($13,650.50 and rising) stay on the table. The email did not
lose the case; it drew the boundary of the case. Conceding $32,000 we cannot win is what makes the
other $398,550.50 credible.

## 2. I will not assert the interference claim — and their lawyer has already written its obituary

Not a style preference. A lawyer may not bring a claim that is objectively groundless (R. 3.1,
`library/fee_shifting_and_candor.md`), and on our own library every element is dead on paper:

- **A party cannot interfere with its own contract** — *Ashby v. Caldera Partners*, 212 Col. App. 3d
  440 (2019), quoted in their draft motion (`documents/session2/draft_motion_to_dismiss_excerpt.md`).
- **Consent:** the hires were announced to us in advance; Marcus answered them. Their Ex. 1 exists.
- **Justification is a complete defense** (Restatement §§766, 767, 773): Pinecrest's corporate
  directive to cut agency spend is a legitimate business purpose, and it is *their own* Ex. 2.
- **The $410,000 number is gone anyway:** contract §6 excludes consequential damages — lost
  agency revenue is precisely that.
- Worse, pleading it would require repeating "Juniper never consented to any direct hire," which
  our own records contradict. A demand letter is not a filing, but "a misstatement in it that is
  repeated in a pleading" is a statement to the tribunal (R. 3.3, same library).

And it is not neutral — it is expensive. Their 12(b)(6)-style motion is **already drafted**, and
Exh. C.3 shifts prevailing-party fees. The interference count is the one issue in this case where
*we* would be paying *their* lawyers (`library/faa_2_4.md`). A claim whose only reliable output is
a check from Juniper to Pinecrest is not a weapon. If you direct us to assert it regardless, we
must decline; you would be entitled to retain other counsel to try.

## 3. What actually scares Hanna Lindqvist — use that instead

- **Her own letter promised $265,200 in writing** ("will pay the undisputed balance"). We publish
  that promise back to her and set a payment date. The four invoices are "deemed accepted" under §2 —
  this is a collection motion, not a fight.
- **C.3 runs both ways.** Right now Pinecrest is holding $265,200 it admits it owes while accruing
  **$3,369.00/month** interest. If they keep that up and we file clean contract claims, *they* are
  the side facing a fee award.
- **The $80,000 fee figure is a floor, and they know it.** Priya, Luis and Anne were RNs (hire list
  in the file). Actual first-year RN pay likely exceeds the $64,000 average we used; their own
  payroll holds the numbers, and the reply letter demands them. The fee number can move **up**.
- **"Poaching" is exactly what §3 prices:** 25% of first-year comp per hire, 12 months. You
  bargained for a price on this conduct; that price *is* the remedy. Five hires × $16,000 floor is
  the bargained-for fix — and unlike the tort, it wins.

**Recommendation:** approve the reply letter as drafted (`session2/reply_letter.md` — no tort, no
court threat, Nov. 13 deadline, AAA thereafter). Scared is what an AAA demand on invoices they've
already promised to pay does. A tort count they've already briefed does the opposite.
