Bottom line: **you will not get paid first by filing papers. You get paid by (a) qualifying $78,100 as a
post-petition-priority "20-day goods" administrative claim, and (b) being named a critical vendor by Redwood and
the court.** Right now the biggest risk to that outcome is our own collection department, and the second biggest is

## 1. What Mesa must stop doing today, and why

**Stop, effective immediately and in writing to every person in AR and credit:**

1. **No more dunning letters, emails or calls** to Redwood asking for money.
2. **Turn off the machine, not just the people.** Frank says the March 12 "FINAL NOTICE" was generated automatically — the system "send[s] final notices to everyone who's 30 days late" **[client]**. This account is 40+ days late, so the system will keep firing. **Suppress the Redwood account in the dunning/collections system today**, and check today whether any automated notice, statement or credit-bureau report went out after March 10 — each one is a separate act to collect (library/bc_362.md).
3. **No credit-bureau reporting** and no threats to report.
4. **No threats to hold shipments as leverage** for the old balance. (Deciding prospectively not to sell without
   current terms is a commercial decision; demanding payment of the pre-filing balance in exchange for shipping is
   collection activity.)
5. **No liens, no lawsuits, no garnishment, no offsetting** of deposits or credit balances against the old balance.
   Setting off pre-bankruptcy mutual debts requires the court's permission (library/bc_502d_setoff.md).
6. **Do not accept any money from Redwood** on the old invoices. A payment of a pre-filing debt without court
   authority can be undone, and it would put us back in the preference line of fire.
7. **All contact goes through counsel** to Redwood's counsel. Nobody from AR talks to Redwood AP again — and that
   includes the CEO taking supply-and-payment calls himself (see §4; it has already happened once **[client]**).
8. **Preserve everything** — the collection log, the terms email, wire records, phone records. Litigation hold
   today. Do not "tidy up" the log.

**Why.** When Redwood filed, an automatic stay went into effect against everyone. It stops "any act to collect,
assess, or recover a claim against the debtor that arose before" the filing, and the library says flatly that
"dunning letters, collection calls and threats to report to credit bureaus after the petition are acts to collect"
(library/bc_362.md). A creditor who **willfully** violates the stay pays actual damages, costs and attorneys' fees,
and punitive damages in appropriate circumstances; the library also notes courts **sanction corporate creditors**
under §105 (the §362(k) damages text refers to an "individual" — Redwood is a company, so the §105 route is the one
that bites here).

We have already done three of these things, and they are documented in our own collection log:

| Date | Act | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-03-12 | "FINAL NOTICE — pay **$210,000** within 5 days or we refer to collections" (mailed + emailed), two days after the filing | Stay violation. Worse, the number was wrong: we invoiced $210,000 since 2026-01-12 but **$85,000 was already paid**, so the true open balance was $125,000. We demanded money twice over. |
| 2026-03-15 | Collection call to Redwood AP; we were told on the call "we filed Chapter 11, talk to counsel"; **our own AR clerk** replied, per our log, "that's not our problem, you owe us" | High risk: this is knowledge of the case plus a continued demand. (Checked after Round 1: the "you owe us" line is *our clerk's*, not a Redwood counterclaim — nothing in the file shows Redwood claiming against us.) Frank says Lori called twice **[client]** but the log shows one call — get the phone records. |
| 2026-03-18 | Email to Redwood's CFO threatening to "hold all future shipments and report to credit bureaus" | Highest risk: credit-bureau threats are named in the library as acts to collect, and it was sent after we knew about the case. |

The March 12 letter is at least partly explained: Frank says it was **generated automatically** by the dunning system that final-notices every 30-day-late account **[client]**. That is mitigating on the "willful" element — which the library does not define **[unverified]** — but it does not undo the act, and an unattended machine is no defence to the *next* letter: if the system fires again while the case is pending and known, that is materially worse. Suppress the account today (§1 item 2). March 15 and 18 are not defensible on knowledge: on the 15th Redwood's AP told our caller on the call that they had filed Chapter 11, and our clerk pushed the demand anyway. Damages here are usually attorneys' fees and a sanction, not $125,000 — but it is a real, self-inflicted cost, it is exactly the kind of thing that makes a debtor refuse to make us a critical vendor, and it gives the estate leverage in the preference negotiation discussed next.

The single most valuable thing we can do today is send a written stand-down instruction and keep it. That is the
difference between "our AR clerk lost control of one account" and "Mesa's management condoned collection after
notice."

## 3. What Mesa may owe Redwood (yes, owe)

In a bankruptcy the estate can claw back payments a supplier received in the **90 days before filing** as
"preferences" — 2025-12-10 through 2026-03-10 in our case. Insolvency in that window is presumed against Redwood, so
we assume that element goes against us (library/bc_547.md).

We received **$180,000** in that window:

| Payment | Amount | Days after invoice | Method | Our read |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| 2025-12-18 | $51,000 | 45 | ACH | **Protected** — normal for us |
| 2026-01-22 | $44,000 | 43 | ACH | **Protected** — normal for us |
| 2026-02-10 | $48,200 | 29 | ACH | **Exposed** — after we cut terms to NET 15 |
| 2026-02-27 | $36,800 | 25 | **WIRE we demanded** | **Exposed** — pressure + unusual method |

Our whole prior year was 43–47 days after invoice, always ACH, on net-45 terms. Then our credit department sent the
2026-01-20 email moving Redwood to **NET 15** and demanding "oldest open invoice first". The library is direct:
payments made under shortened terms, pressure, or unusual methods (wire on demand) compared with the parties' history
generally fall outside the ordinary-course defence (library/bc_547.md). So roughly **$95,000 protected / $85,000
exposed**. And we carry the burden of proving those defences, not Redwood (library/bc_547.md §547(g)).

The good news is the **new value** defence: after those two dangerous payments we kept shipping on credit —
$99,400 of goods and services (2026-02-20 onward) that Redwood never paid for. New value given after a payment and
not paid for reduces preference exposure dollar for dollar (library/bc_547.md §547(c)(4)). $99,400 against $85,000 of
exposure means the honest estimate of what we owe is **about zero**.

Lori's Round-1 account **confirms** this reading **[client]**: "In January I cut them to net-15 and made them pay the oldest invoice before I'd ship" (that is the Feb 10 payment), and for the Feb 27 wire, "I told them no steel until it hit our account." That is the estate's best evidence against the two February payments — do not expect the $95,000/$85,000 split to move, and do not let anyone testify that those payments were "business as usual."

Three caveats, stated as they are:
- If a court refuses to let the same 20-day goods count both as a §503(b)(9) administrative claim *and* as new value,
  exposure rises to about **$63,700**. The library does not address that question **[unverified]**.
- **New problem from Lori's own words:** because we demanded payment *before* each new truck of steel, a court could
  treat the Feb 10 and Feb 27 payments as made "on account of" the later shipments, which would collapse the new-value
  credit entirely and put exposure back at **$85,000**. The library quotes the new-value rule but does not cover that
  argument **[unverified]**.
- Planning ceiling if both defences fail: **$85,000** (the two February payments). If somehow nothing is defended:
  $180,000.

**Reserve $85,000 — not $63,700 — until counsel settles the new-value question.** That is the number the client's own
story supports, and it is also the number the estate will open with inside its realistic range.

Why this matters even if we win: under §502(d), a creditor from whom a preference is recoverable can have its **entire
claim disallowed** until it pays the preference (library/bc_502d_setoff.md). An unresolved preference fight can freeze
our $125,000 — including the $78,100 administrative piece. So the preference file should be prepared now, with the
payment history, the delivery records and a witness who can explain the terms change, not in eight months.

Practical posture: expect the estate to open near $180,000, argue us down into the **$0–$85,000** range once they see
the delivery records, and — because their counsel has already floated keeping us supplied for payment of the old
invoices **[client]** — expect the preference claim and the critical-vendor order to be negotiated **together**, not
in separate conversations. Whoever answers both conversations must be the same lawyer.

## 4. The critical-vendor path — the only legitimate way to get paid early

Being named a **critical vendor** is the sanctioned route to early payment of pre-filing claims: the debtor asks the
court to pay certain vendors whose continued supply is essential and who cannot be replaced, and the vendor normally
has to agree to customary trade terms during the case. It is at the **debtor's discretion with the court's
approval** — we cannot force it by filing something (library/critical_vendor_practice.md, citing *In re Kmart Corp.*,
359 F.3d 866 (7th Cir. 2004), and Dkt. 44 in this case).

What the notice tells us: the critical-vendor motion is **Dkt. 44, hearing 2026-04-02**, and "creditors wishing to be
considered must contact Debtor's counsel" (documents/notice_of_commencement_and_bar_date.pdf). So:

1. **Redwood's counsel has already approached us.** Frank reports they asked whether Mesa would keep supplying on
   normal terms if the old invoices are paid, and he answered "pay first" — "I didn't know there was a motion"
   **[client]**. Their question *is* the critical-vendor bargain (Dkt. 44, hearing 2026-04-02), and "pay first" is the
   opposite of the customary-terms offer the court expects (library/critical_vendor_practice.md). **Counsel must call
   their counsel before April 2**, reset the answer, and put a written supply-terms proposal on the table. We are a
   sole-or-primary steel source on an account that has run at least twelve months — say so, with specifics on lead
   times and who else could supply them.
2. **Offer what they need:** normal trade terms for the case (e.g. net-30/45 on current shipments, no cash-on-demand
   threats), continued supply, and no collection activity. That offer is also our answer to the preference fight —
   "we kept shipping unsecured after the payments" is the new value defence.
3. **Do not tie supply to the old money.** "Pay us the $125,000 or we stop shipping" is (i) a stay problem, and
   (ii) the fastest way to be left off the list. Frank's "pay first" answer must not be repeated by anyone **[client]**.
4. Post-filing shipments are a different animal and should be paid as current costs; the library contains no
   authority on payment for post-filing deliveries **[unverified]**, so any new shipments must be on terms confirmed
   with debtor's counsel and covered by the DIP lender's order. Our last delivery was 2026-03-09, so we are already
   dark — which weakens the "essential supplier" story. If continuing to supply is commercially right, formalise it
   now; if not, say so to the client file, because it changes what we ask the court for.
5. Do not expect a critical-vendor order to pay 100% to a supplier who is also a preference target. Expect a
   negotiated number: partial pre-filing payment in exchange for new shipments on ordinary terms and a mutual
   preference stand-down.
