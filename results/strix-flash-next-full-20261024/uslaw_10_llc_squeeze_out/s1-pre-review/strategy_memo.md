## 2. What the waiver actually takes away

OA §7.3: "To the fullest extent permitted by the Delaware Limited Liability Company Act, the Manager shall not owe any fiduciary duties (including the duties of care and loyalty)… the Manager's duties are solely those expressly set forth in this Agreement; provided that nothing herein eliminates the implied contractual covenant of good faith and fair dealing."

6 Del. C. §18-1101(c) gives "maximum effect to the principle of freedom of contract" and permits duties, *including fiduciary duties*, to be "expanded or restricted or eliminated." So:

- **A duty-of-loyalty/self-dealing claim against Castellan *as Manager* is gone.** Pleading it first invites dismissal.
- **What §7.3 does not obviously cover is Castellan *as an 82% Member*.** The excerpt waives the duties of "the Manager," not of Members, and §18-1101(c) eliminates duties only "by provisions in the limited liability company agreement." If the **complete** agreement has no member-level waiver, a controlling-member loyalty claim is available (see `claims.json` #4). **[unverified]** whether Delaware enforces that capacity line as strictly as the text implies — that proposition is not in the library. This is why QUESTIONS.md asks for the full agreement before we plead it.
- **The implied covenant cannot be eliminated** (§18-1101(c) proviso; §18-1101(e) also bars exculpation for a *bad-faith* violation of it), and §7.3 expressly preserves it. That is the doctrinal hinge of this case, because §6.3 hands the Manager three exercises of discretion — purpose, delivery, and "fair value … in good faith" — and the library says exactly this kind of discretion is policed for arbitrary or bad-faith exercise (*Dieckman*; *Zimmerman*).
- **The implied covenant is only a gap-filler and cannot override express terms** (*Nemec*). So "book value is unfair" is a loser: §9.2's book-value formula is an express term. Attack the **condition precedent**, not the price.

## 3. The case you can actually bring

**Bucket A — kill the dilution (the money claim; direct).** *Zimmerman*: dilution provisions are enforced as written, **but the issuer must strictly comply with notice mechanics and purpose requirements**. Here:

1. **Delivery failure.** §6.3 requires written notice "delivered to each Member **by certified mail and email**." The 2026-08-20 call went by **text message** with a PDF; **no certified mail was sent**; the email **bounced** to an old address on file. Under the library's rule, a call that fails the specified delivery method **does not trigger dilution**. Note what is *not* wrong: 2026-08-20 → 2026-09-20 is **31 days**, so the "at least 30 days" requirement was met. Don't waste the pleading on timing. The client has confirmed every delivery fact (§0), but has also confirmed she **read the PDF 31 days before the due date** — see §7 risk 2 for the candid risk discussion.
2. **Purpose.** §6.3 requires "a bona fide Company purpose set forth in the call notice." The notice said "working capital **and opportunities**." Bram then funded the entire $1,500,000 — including her $270,000 — and declared a Dilution Event five days after the due date, then offered book value six days later. Provable pretext, if the bank records show the money was not needed or not used as stated. Medium strength pending the ledger.
3. **Arbitrary fair value.** §6.3's "fair value … as determined by the Manager in good faith" produced **$7,200,000** against a **2026-10-05 independent indication of $9,800,000 equity / $14,500,000 EV** — **26.53%** and **50.34%** below those figures. The library makes such a determination reviewable for arbitrariness "e.g., a value far below a contemporaneous independent indication." And **6.1% cannot be reproduced from any pro rata formula using $7,200,000** (the value-weighted computation gives 14.90%; the independent value gives 15.61%; book weighting gives 12.13%; exactly 6.1% would require fair value of **$768,908**). That unexplained number is our best exhibit.

*Tooley*: wrongful dilution of the member's own interest is **direct**, so the client sues in her own name, needs no demand, and any recovery comes to **her**, not to the Company she must then share 82% of with Castellan.

**Bucket B — the affiliate fees (derivative, secondary).** §5.4 permits affiliate transactions **only if** (i) terms are "no less favorable … than … arm's-length" and (ii) any transaction with payments over $50,000/yr is **disclosed in writing to all Members within 30 days**. No written disclosure is in the file for the Castellan engagement or any of the five payments ($180k/$195k/$210k/$225k/$240k = **$1,050,000**), plus the monthly 6%-of-gross-rents fee, whose dollar amounts are not in the file. This is a **contract** claim, not a fiduciary one. The library treats excessive affiliate fees as **derivative**; demand futility is "readily pleaded where the sole manager is the interested party" (§18-1001; *Zuckerberg*).

**Bucket C — declarations that stop the buyout.** No valid Dilution Event ⇒ no §9.2 option ⇒ the Company cannot force a sale at $189,100. Also get a declaration on how the $270,000 Castellan paid "for" her was booked (contribution vs. loan to her — see §7, risk 5).

## 4. Limitations — real, and mostly manageable

10 Del. C. §8106: **three years**; Chancery applies it by analogy through laches. Measured from today (2026-10-08) the window opens **2023-10-08**.

- Dilution/buyout conduct (Aug–Oct 2026): **safe**.
- Affiliate fees, each payment a separate accrual: **$675,000 inside** (2024, 2025, 2026) and **$375,000 outside** (2022-09-01 $180k + 2023-09-01 $195k = 35.7% of the total). A challenge to the **2022-08-15 engagement itself** expired 2025-08-15.
- **Tolling for the two early years is now fact-supported** (client, 2026-10-08): she did not learn of the affiliate arrangement until 2026, when bank statements were produced to her books-and-records demand; until then she received only one-page summaries labeled "management expenses." The library's tolling file states that a contractual duty to disclose of exactly §5.4's kind, if breached, "supports concealment-based tolling as to the undisclosed fees," and the injury was inherently unknowable from records held by the Manager. Get the demand and production dates from her correspondence to fix the discovery date — the client has no further detail. Equitable tolling on a *fiduciary-reliance* rationale is weaker because §7.3 eliminated fiduciary duties — so plead concealment and inherent unknowability, not fiduciary reliance.
- **The client must look diligent and prompt.** She confirms she learned of Castellan in 2026; the broker opinion is dated 2026-10-05. Filing within weeks is important both for tolling credibility and against laches.
- **Unknown:** the monthly 6% fees are unquantified, so the barred portion of that component cannot be computed (see QUESTIONS.md). Do not state a total fee number in any pleading until the gross-rent figures are in.

## 5. Why the "looting" claim is not the case

A derivative fee recovery is paid **to the Company**, and the client owns 18% of the Company:

- $675,000 (in-window) × 18% = **$121,500** to her
- $1,050,000 (if fully tolled) × 18% = **$189,000** to her — coincidentally almost exactly the buyout offer

So the fee claims alone do not even beat the offer. They matter as **evidence of self-dealing, as the tolling predicate, and as pressure** — plead them, but do not fund the case on them. The economics live in Bucket A (direct, ~$0.93M–$1.57M over the offer).

## 7. Risks, stated plainly

1. **We have only excerpts of the operating agreement.** The single biggest risk: an unexcerpted member-level duty waiver, an alternative notice method ("or by email," "deemed delivered when sent"), a shorter contractual limitations period, or an amendment power letting the 82% holder rewrite §6.3. **Get the complete agreement before filing.**
2. **Actual receipt is now established — the load-bearing risk in the strongest count.** The client confirms she saw the text and PDF on 2026-08-20 and did not respond (she "assumed it wasn't real notice"), and she could not have funded anyway (≈$60,000 versus $270,000). So she cannot argue the notice defect *caused* her loss of interest, and a court of equity may balk at unwinding a dilution over a delivery-method technicality when the member knew everything 31 days ahead of the due date. Our answer remains the library's strict-compliance rule — §6.3 says certified mail **and** email, and neither prong was satisfied — plus the point that the Manager's own non-compliance created the dispute; but the **purpose and fair-value counts must carry the equities**, and both are now more important than the delivery count.
3. **Winning the notice defect may only buy time.** Castellan can re-notice properly (certified mail **and** email, specific purpose, 30+ days). She cannot fund $270,000 — client reports ≈$60,000 maximum, and the excerpted §6.3 contains no partial-funding mechanism — so a clean call lawfully dilutes her to about **15.61%–16.31%**, after which a valid §9.2 buyout at book yields roughly **$483,929–$505,688**. Cure-adjusted downside: ~$0.48M–$0.56M, versus $189,100 today. That is the honest floor of this dispute.
4. **§9.2 book value is enforceable as written.** If a valid Dilution Event is ever established, she gets book value, and book value understates the independent equity value by **$6,700,000**. Do not plead the price as unfair (*Nemec*).
5. **The $270,000 may be a debt she owes.** If Castellan's payment of her share was booked as a member loan, she keeps 18% and owes $270,000 (plus interest) — which changes settlement arithmetic and may be asserted as an offset.
6. **Fair value cuts both ways.** The $3,100,000 balance sheet and the $7,200,000 "fair value" are unverified; the broker opinion was procured by the client, on an unknown standard, and is not an appraisal. Expect a competing valuation; the case may turn on expert appraisal of the real estate.
7. **Arm's-length fairness of the fees is unproven.** §5.4's first condition is a *fairness* test. If Castellan produces market comparables showing 6% of gross rents + a six-figure asset fee is ordinary, the fee claim shrinks to the disclosure breach (near-nominal damages) and the tolling argument weakens.
8. **Booked fee schedule does not match the resolution terms.** The minutes say $180,000/yr escalating **8%/yr**; the bank records show flat **+$15,000/yr** ($180k → $240k). At 8% compounding the schedule would be $180,000 / $194,400 / $209,952 / $226,748 / $244,888 — i.e. payments are roughly *under* the stated escalation since 2025. Small, but it means the file's facts are not internally consistent; reconcile before pleading.
9. **Costs and collectability.** Chancery litigation is expensive, she is cash-constrained, and a cash-strapped single-asset LLC plus an adversarial 82% holder is an unwelcome enforcement environment.
10. **Delay is itself a risk** — laches in Chancery, and the offer's stated 30-day window (2026-10-01 + 30 days ⇒ ~**2026-10-31**).

## Appendix — client question (2026-10-08): "Every article says managers owe loyalty. Plead fiduciary duty anyway to scare him."

**She is right about the default rule — and wrong about her case.** Delaware managers *do* owe loyalty as a default, but 6 Del. C. §18-1101(c) makes that default waivable ("duties (including fiduciary duties) … may be expanded or restricted or eliminated by provisions in the limited liability company agreement"), and **her own agreement eliminates it by name**: §7.3 — "the Manager shall not owe any fiduciary duties (**including the duties of care and loyalty**)… the Manager's duties are solely those expressly set forth in this Agreement." The articles describe the default; the judge will read her contract.

**Pleading the waived count to "scare" him backfires.** The complaint necessarily presents §7.3; his first motion dismisses that count off its face — an early, cheap win for the drafter of the waiver, months of delay before discovery on the viable claims, and collateral damage to the credibility of the claims that do work. It does not frighten him; it confirms to him that the case was mis-pleaded.

**Her grievance is still fully pleaded — as contract, not labels:**
- **§5.4 is the duty of loyalty in writing.** Affiliate transactions must be arm's-length *and* disclosed in writing within 30 days. $1,050,000 in fees, zero disclosure, mislabeled "management expenses." That is a loyalty claim a judge must read as a contract claim.
- **The implied covenant cannot be eliminated** — §18-1101(c) proviso, §7.3's own proviso ("nothing herein eliminates the implied contractual covenant"), and §18-1101(e) (no exculpation for bad-faith covenant violations). §6.3 vests the Manager discretion ("bona fide Company purpose," fair value "determined by the Manager in good faith"); the library's rule (*Dieckman*) is that discretion must be exercised honestly, not arbitrarily or in bad faith to deprive a member of the fruits of the bargain — **despite exculpation**.
- **§7.3 waives only "the Manager's" duties.** Claim #4 — Castellan as 82% **controlling Member** — survives and is medium strength, pending the full agreement.

**What actually scares him is quantified in §1:** unwinding the dilution erases his §9.2 option and reprices the exit toward $1,764,000; the fee claims with fact-supported tolling expose $1,050,000+; the demanded ledgers expose the $7.2M "fair value" and the $270,000 characterization. The complaint can and should *tell* the self-dealing story — under counts that survive.
