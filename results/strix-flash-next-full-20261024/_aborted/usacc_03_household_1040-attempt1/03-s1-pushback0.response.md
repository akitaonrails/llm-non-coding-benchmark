Fair question — here's the plain-English answer, straight from the rule in our reference set.

**Why $16,500 is suspended**

Marcus's K-1 loss is $28,000, but an S-corp shareholder can only deduct a loss up to the amount he has "at risk" in the stock — his basis. The basis trail from Marcus's own note, which he confirmed this year:

| | |
|---|---:|
| Put in at formation (2021) | +10,000 |
| 2021 loss | −2,100 |
| 2022 income | +6,400 |
| 2023 income − distributions | +9,800 − 12,000 |
| 2024 income − distributions | +4,600 − 5,200 |
| **Basis entering 2025** | **11,500** |

Loss allowed = lesser of the loss ($28,000) and basis ($11,500) → **$11,500 allowed, $16,500 suspended** — exactly Form 7203's language: "losses and deductions are allowed **only to the extent of** adjusted stock basis plus debt basis" ([form7203_instructions_excerpt.md]).

**The $16,500 is not lost.** It carries forward **indefinitely** and pops loose later — for example, if Reyes & Pike turns a profit in 2026, that profit first restores Marcus's basis, and the suspended loss then becomes deductible against it. The buddy's "whole loss" story almost certainly means the buddy *had* the basis: bigger contributions, prior income building, or a loan the shareholder made **directly to the corporation**. Those people can deduct 100% too — basis is what varies, not the rule.

**Pike's guarantee doesn't help Marcus**

This part is black-and-white, and the library says it in one sentence: "**A guarantee of corporate debt does not create debt basis; only a direct loan from the shareholder to the corporation does**." Two independent problems:

1. A *guarantee* gives the guarantor nothing until he actually pays the bank — and even then the basis belongs to **the person who paid**, which would be Pike, not Marcus;
2. Marcus isn't the guarantor anyway — Marcus himself confirmed "I never lent it money. Pike guarantees the bank line." And his $68,000 W-2 wages from the S corp don't add basis either; wages are compensation, not a contribution.

**What *could* legitimately change this** (for 2026 planning, not 2025): Marcus lending money to the corporation **directly** (paper it as a shareholder loan, not a guarantee), making a capital contribution, or the company going profitable. Any of those releases the suspended $16,500. If Marcus believes he ever *did* lend directly to Reyes & Pike, tell me and I'll rework it — but he's already answered no, so the filed figures stand: **$11,500 allowed, $16,500 carried forward on Form 7203**.
