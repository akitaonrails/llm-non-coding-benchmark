## 3. Which counting rules apply to which deadline

**State-court answer — Washington rules, Washington holidays.** CR 12(a)(1): answer within **20 days after service**; CR 6(a): exclude the day of service, count every intervening day, and extend only if the **last** day is a Saturday, Sunday, or *Washington* legal holiday under **RCW 1.16.050**. The summons itself tracks that count — "SUMMONS (20 DAYS) … within 20 days after the service of this summons, **excluding the day of service**." Per the library, **Washington does not observe Columbus Day**; state courts are open Mon. Oct. 12, 2026 (`library/wa_cr_4_6_12.md`, `library/rcw_1_16_050_wa_holidays.md`). **No CR 6(e) 3-day mail addition** — that rule covers papers served under CR 5, not the summons served under CR 4, and this was personal delivery on the registered agent. The CR 4(a)(2) 60-day figure (out-of-state service by publication / RCW 4.28.185) is inapplicable to personal service on a registered agent, even in Delaware. A "20 + 3 = Oct. 4 → Oct. 5" computation is **wrong**.

**Assumption (flagged again in § 9):** we treat plaintiff's Delaware service on Corporation Service Company as legally effective and will not contest service. The library's CR 4 excerpt does not reproduce the out-of-state service provisions; its parenthetical ("not applicable to personal service on a registered agent") shows the rule contemplates that mode of service, and the federal analogue, FRCP 4(h), treats delivery to "any other agent authorized by appointment or by law to receive service of process" as service on the entity — which is what the declaration describes. If the service were defective the 20-day clock would not have started Sept. 11; nothing in the file suggests that, but the rule that makes this service effective is outside the closed library.

**Removal (for completeness only) — federal rules, federal holidays.** 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b)(1): 30 days after receipt "through service or otherwise" of a copy of the initial pleading. Fed. R. Civ. P. 6(a)(1)(B) counts intermediate weekends/holidays; 6(a)(1)(C) rolls the **last** day if it is a weekend or legal holiday; Rule 6(a)(6)(A) includes **Columbus Day**, and the W.D. Wash. Clerk's Office is closed **Mon. Oct. 12, 2026** (`library/frcp_4_5_6_12_excerpts.md`, `library/federal_holidays_2026.md`). Rule 6(d)'s 3 days does not apply: it attaches to Rule 5(b)(2)(C)/(D)/(F) service, and the rule's own note excludes service of the summons and complaint under Rule 4.

*Unverified (library silent):* whether a defendant's later actual knowledge could restart the § 1446(b)(1) clock. Irrelevant here anyway — the client's own answer is that CSC emailed the scans on **Sept. 11**, which is a "receipt … otherwise" date, and service was also Sept. 11.

**Plaintiff's citizenship: Washington only.** A corporation is a citizen of every state of incorporation and of its principal place of business — § 1332(c)(1). Both are Washington (¶ 1).

**Defendant's citizenship: Washington and Oregon.** An LLC is an unincorporated association and "is a citizen of every state of which its owners/members are citizens"; § 1332(c)(1)'s corporation test **does not** apply. *Johnson v. Columbia Properties Anchorage, LP*, 437 F.3d 894, 899 (9th Cir. 2006); *Carden v. Arkoma Associates*, 494 U.S. 185 (1990). An individual is a citizen of the state of domicile, and "a single non-diverse member destroys complete diversity."

| Party | Citizenship on the confirmed facts |
|---|---|
| Sound Cascade Foods, Inc. | **Washington** (incorporation + PPB Kent) — § 1332(c)(1) |
| Harbor Street Bakery LLC | **Washington** (member Caleb Sorensen, lives Seattle-Ballard, 35%) **and Oregon** (member Noor Haddad, lives Portland, 65%) — *Johnson*/*Carden*. Delaware organization and the Portland PPB are irrelevant |
| Others | none joined or served |

**Ground 1 — no complete diversity (§ 1332(a)(1)).** A Washington citizen (Sound Cascade) and a Washington citizen (Harbor Street, through Caleb) are adverse parties; the "citizens of different States" requirement fails. There was never federal subject-matter jurisdiction, so there is nothing to remove under § 1441(a).

**Ground 2 — forum-defendant rule (§ 1441(b)(2)).** Even if the diversity analysis were argued, "a civil action otherwise removable solely on the basis of § 1332(a) may not be removed if any of the parties in interest properly joined and served as defendants is a citizen of the State in which such action is brought." The action was brought in Washington; the only defendant, taking its member's citizenship, is a Washington citizen. Independently fatal.

**Where removal would have gone anyway:** § 1441(a) sends a removed case to "the district and division embracing the place where such action is pending" — W.D. Wash. — not Delaware. The "Delaware company" theory was wrong twice.

**Cost of trying it:** § 1447(c) — a court must remand whenever it appears subject-matter jurisdiction is lacking, and "an order remanding the case may require payment of just costs and any actual expenses, including attorney fees, incurred as a result of the removal." On the confirmed roster that outcome is close to certain, so the fee exposure is real and one-sided. Also, the LLC's operating agreement has not been produced; if it shows an additional member, diversity only gets worse, never better — an LLC takes the citizenship of *all* its members, so added members can only add states; they cannot erase Caleb's Washington. Whether a member that is itself an entity is looked through to its own members is **not addressed in the library** — flagged as unverified (the *Johnson*/*Carden* look-through logic suggests it, but I will not cite it as settled). Either way, Caleb's Washington membership alone defeats complete diversity.

### 5.6 "Are you sure? Delaware LLC vs. Washington corporation is textbook diversity" (your question, Sept. 28)

Your friend is applying a real rule. It is just the wrong rule for your entity. Here is the whole argument, so you can check it.

**Step 1 — his rule is the *corporation* rule.** "State of incorporation + principal place of business" comes from 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1). The library's own note on that provision says it "applies to corporations." For a corporation it is textbook, and it is exactly how Sound Cascade's citizenship is computed: incorporated in Washington, PPB in Kent → Washington (§ 1332(c)(1); complaint ¶ 1).

**Step 2 — Harbor Street is not a corporation, so § 1332(c)(1) does not reach it.** The library is explicit: an unincorporated association — "including a **limited liability company**" — "is a citizen of every state of which its owners/members are citizens," and "the § 1332(c)(1) corporate test (state of incorporation and principal place of business) does **not** apply." *Johnson v. Columbia Properties Anchorage, LP*, 437 F.3d 894, 899 (9th Cir. 2006), puts it in one line: **"like a partnership, an LLC is a citizen of every state of which its owners/members are citizens."** *Carden v. Arkoma Associates*, 494 U.S. 185 (1990), is the same idea for a limited partnership: you look through the entity to the people behind it.

**Step 3 — "Delaware" buys you nothing here; it is not a citizenship at all for an LLC.** Under *Johnson*, an LLC's state of organization is legally irrelevant to diversity. Delaware drops off the map entirely, and so does "principal place of business in Portland." The only states on the diversity map for Harbor Street are the states where its **members** are citizens: Washington (Caleb) and Oregon (you). Compare the two versions of the same facts:

| | Entity form | Diversity citizenship | vs. plaintiff (WA) | Removable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Your friend's assumption | Harbor Street, a Delaware **corporation**, PPB Oregon | Delaware + Oregon | diverse | yes, § 1332(a) |
| Reality | Harbor Street, a Delaware **LLC**, members in WA and OR | **Washington** + Oregon | **not** diverse | **no** |

Same lawsuit, same Delaware paperwork — different answer, purely because of the entity form. That is the whole disagreement.

**Step 4 — "Caleb being in Seattle shouldn't matter; the company isn't him."** For *liability* your instinct is sound: the LLC, not Caleb, is the defendant. (That members are not answerable for an LLC's debts is general entity law — the library says nothing about LLC liability, so I flag that aside as outside the closed record rather than citing it.) For *citizenship* the law runs the other direction: the LLC has no citizenship of its own, so it borrows its members'. That is not a technicality a court will excuse — the library's entry states the consequence flatly: **"a single non-diverse member destroys complete diversity."** One Washington member, 35%, is enough.

**Step 5 — even on his theory, Delaware was never the destination.** § 1441(a) sends a removed case to "the district and division embracing the place where such action is pending." The action is pending in King County, Washington, so the destination is the federal district court in Washington the library refers to (W.D. Wash.) — not Delaware. There is no route that moves this case to a Delaware courthouse.

**Step 6 — the second, independent bar.** Even if diversity somehow existed, § 1441(b)(2) says an action "otherwise removable solely on the basis of § 1332(a) may not be removed if any of the parties in interest properly joined and served as defendants is a citizen of the State in which such action is brought." This suit was brought in Washington, and the defendant is a Washington citizen (through Caleb). So we would be barred twice over.

**The one fact that could change my answer — and why I would still not file.** The test is **domicile**, not residence ("An individual is a citizen of the state of his or her domicile"). If Caleb were domiciled in Oregon — living in Ballard only temporarily, home ties still in Oregon — Harbor Street would be an **Oregon-only** citizen, both bars would disappear, and removal would be live (Oct. 13; that is exactly the scenario in the `if_removed` block). Everything you have told me points the other way: he lives in Seattle (Ballard) and runs the Seattle shop. And if the operating agreement shows a third member, diversity only gets worse, never better — added members can only add citizenships; they cannot erase Caleb's Washington.

So: yes, I am sure, **on the facts you gave me.** What would change it is documented proof that Caleb is domiciled outside Washington, and I would want that before, not after, anything is filed: the removing party carries the risk of being wrong, and § 1447(c) requires remand whenever jurisdiction is lacking and "may require payment of just costs and any actual expenses, including attorney fees, incurred as a result of the removal." That is a one-sided bet on a coin flip, on a case whose value does not change with the courthouse — the ~$84,916.40 residual and the $18,240 butter claim come with the file.

**Do not do any of the following:** file a notice of removal; tell Caleb his address matters and ask him to characterize it; or let this question delay the answer that is due **Thursday, Oct. 1**. If Caleb has facts that already contradict Washington domicile (Oregon driver's license, voter registration, Oregon tax filing, an unbroken Oregon lease), send them to me and I will re-run § 5 — but do not go looking for a favorable answer, and do not treat the question as open in the meantime.

The library's *Johnson* entry is the Ninth Circuit's answer for LLCs, and it is stated as in force.
