# Privileged legal assessment — Meridian Robotics / Wei-Lin Zhao

**As of October 22, 2026.** Closed-library assessment for the GC. The probability estimates below are judgment ranges, not a statistical model.

## Question Presented

Should Meridian sue in Travis County to enforce §8 of its March 4, 2024 employment agreement, a 24-month, nationwide bar on work for any warehouse autonomous mobile robot business, against Zhao, who lived and worked in California and has joined a California competitor?

## Brief Answer

**No.** I estimate only a **5–15% chance of obtaining a practically effective court order restricting Zhao's Kestrel employment**, and less than 5% for enforcement of §8 as written. Those ranges account for uncertainty over a Texas court's treatment of the forum clause, conflicts law, and possible reformation; they do not imply that these events are independent or measurable from the client file. California has the stronger claim to govern Zhao's employment restraint. Zhao can elect to void the Texas forum and law provisions under Cal. Lab. Code §925, and California would void §8 under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600. Texas also applies a materially greater interest/fundamental policy conflicts analysis; the Texas choice clause is therefore not a reliable route around California law. Even under Texas substantive law, the nationwide, all-role, 24-month ban appears broader than the facts support. A Texas court could reform it, but that may produce little useful relief in California. Suing or threatening to enforce §8 also creates affirmative California statutory exposure, including Zhao's actual damages if proved and mandatory fees if he prevails under §16600.5.

## Facts and limits of the record

Meridian sent Zhao an offer on February 28, 2024, for a fully remote California role. The March 4 agreement identifies Oakland as the work location. Zhao signed a standard form **113 minutes** after receipt (11:05 a.m. − 9:12 a.m. = 1 hour 53 minutes), without negotiation or counsel. He worked from Oakland through his September 30, 2026 resignation, apart from two Austin trips (March 2024 onboarding and an October 2025 offsite). His new Kestrel Autonomy position began October 13 in Fremont. On September 16 he wrote that California counsel considers §8 void and that he may void the Texas forum and law clauses. He promised to honor confidentiality obligations and take no company material. The file contains no evidence of taking information, solicitation, misuse, loss, Zhao's exact duties at Kestrel, or the reach of his Meridian work. These are evidentiary gaps, not adverse facts found against either party.

Section 8 bars employment or services for any United States business engaged in design or sale of warehouse autonomous mobile robots for 24 months after termination, regardless of job function. Its stated term runs from September 30, 2026 to September 30, 2028 (**24 months = 2 years**). Section 9 is a separate 12-month employee/customer nonsolicit, ending September 30, 2027 (**12 months = 1 year**); this memo does not opine on its enforceability. Section 10 covers confidentiality and inventions. Section 14 chooses Texas law and exclusive state or federal venue in Travis County. Section 15 says Zhao had an opportunity to consult counsel, but the HR record establishes he was not in fact represented in negotiating those terms.

## Analysis

### Forum and governing law

Texas has a real connection: Meridian's Austin headquarters sent the offer, Zhao visited twice, and the parties chose Texas. That likely satisfies the substantial-relationship threshold for an initial Texas law choice. But Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §187(2) allows displacement of a chosen law where applying it would defeat the fundamental policy of a state with a materially greater interest that would supply law absent the clause. California applies that framework in *Nedlloyd Lines B.V. v. Superior Court*, 3 Cal.4th 459 (1992). *Application Group, Inc. v. Hunter Group, Inc.*, 61 Cal.App.4th 881 (1998), identifies §16600 as a fundamental California policy and displaced another state's chosen law in a noncompete dispute involving California employment. Texas's *DeSantis v. Wackenhut Corp.*, 793 S.W.2d 670 (Tex. 1990), likewise rejected a chosen state's noncompete law where the employee's work state had the materially greater interest and contrary fundamental policy. These cases do not decide this exact Texas/California fact pattern. Here, however, California is where Zhao lived, performed substantially all work, and now competes; its interest is materially greater for the restraint issue. Without an effective choice, those contacts strongly favor California law. A Texas court must still decide the conflicts question; filing in Travis County does not guarantee Texas substantive law.

Cal. Lab. Code §925 provides an additional, direct objection to §14. Zhao primarily resided and worked in California, signed in 2024 as a condition of employment on this record, and the prospective noncompete dispute arises from California employment and future work. Section 925(a) prohibits terms requiring out-of-state adjudication of a California claim or loss of California substantive protection; §925(b) makes those terms **voidable at the employee's request**, with California adjudication and law if voided. The counsel exception requires actual individual representation in negotiating the forum or law terms, §925(e). A boilerplate opportunity-to-consult acknowledgment is not that. Zhao's email says he *may* void the terms; it is not clearly an exercise of the statutory election. Expect him to make that request if Meridian files. Section 925 applies because this agreement was entered after January 1, 2017, §925(f). Its consequences are strong but a Texas court's handling of a California statutory voiding election remains a litigation risk, not a guaranteed procedural outcome.

The forum clause ordinarily supports consent to personal jurisdiction in Texas. If Zhao voids it, Meridian would need an independent jurisdiction basis; a Texas-employer contract and two short visits may or may not suffice on this record. A Texas injunction against California work would also face recognition difficulties in California. The library's Texas jurisdiction practice note flags both risks (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §17.042; *Michiana Easy Livin' Country v. Holten*, 168 S.W.3d 777 (Tex. 2005)). *Advanced Bionics Corp. v. Medtronic, Inc.*, 29 Cal.4th 697 (2002), cautions that California courts generally should not enjoin parallel litigation in another state's courts; it does **not** make the Texas forum or noncompete enforceable here.

### If California law applies

Section 16600 voids employment noncompetes outside statutory exceptions, however narrowly drafted; *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP*, 44 Cal.4th 937 (2008), rejected the former narrow-restraint theory. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600(a)–(b). The agreement contains no fact suggesting a statutory exception such as a sale transaction. Section 8 directly bars Zhao's lawful occupation, including work at Kestrel, and is void to that extent. Its scope makes the result clearer but is not necessary to it. *Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc.*, 9 Cal.5th 1130 (2020), applies a rule of reason to business-to-business restraints and expressly distinguishes employment; it does not rescue this covenant. Earlier narrow-restraint reasoning in *Campbell v. Board of Trustees*, 817 F.2d 499 (9th Cir. 1987), and *General Commercial Packaging, Inc. v. TPS Package Engineering, Inc.*, 126 F.3d 1131 (9th Cir. 1997), was disapproved by *Edwards* and is not current California law. The library's older internal memos contain case citations outside the closed library; their propositions are unverified and do not change this assessment.

Section 16600.5(a) makes a covenant void under California law unenforceable regardless of where and when signed; (b) forbids an employer's attempt to enforce it even where contracting and employment were outside California. Zhao's California signing, residence, work, and new job present an especially direct California nexus. Meridian's March 2024 execution of the agreement also implicates §16600.5(c)–(d), which prohibit entering a contract with a void provision and characterize entry or attempted enforcement as a civil violation. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600.5. A demand letter invoking §8 or a Texas enforcement suit could supply the attempted-enforcement theory. The existing agreement is already a potential entry violation; this memo cannot quantify any resulting loss or remedy without more facts.

### If Texas substantive law applies

Texas requires a covenant ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement and reasonable as to time, geography, and activity, without greater restraint than needed to protect goodwill or another business interest. For a personal-services employment agreement, Meridian bears the burden of proving these elements. Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §15.50(a), §15.51(b). Confidentiality promises and any confidential information actually supplied may help Meridian establish an otherwise enforceable arrangement. *Alex Sheshunoff Management Services, L.P. v. Johnson*, 209 S.W.3d 644 (Tex. 2006), permits an employer's promised confidential-information consideration to support an at-will covenant upon performance, even after signing. The record has a confidentiality clause but does not establish what information was supplied. *Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook*, 354 S.W.3d 764 (Tex. 2011), recognizes protection of goodwill where stock-option consideration related to it; no stock-option fact appears here. Neither case dispenses with reasonableness.

Meridian has a legitimate concern that a senior perception engineer could bring sensitive know-how to a direct AMR competitor. But §8 covers every United States location and any job at any business in the described sector for two years, with no tie to Zhao's customer relationships, engineering duties, confidential-information access, or actual competitive geography. The record provides no national remit or other basis for that breadth. A Texas court would likely find at least geography and activity overbroad. If Texas law governs and the threshold ancillary-agreement requirement is met, §15.51(c) directs a court to reform unreasonable limits and enforce the reformed covenant; Meridian cannot recover damages for a breach before reformation. Section 15.52 makes the Act's enforceability criteria and remedies exclusive. Reformation is a meaningful argument for Meridian, but its scope and practical effect against a California employee remain uncertain. The outdated timing restriction attributed to *Light v. Centel Cellular Co. of Texas*, 883 S.W.2d 642 (Tex. 1994), was modified by *Sheshunoff* and cannot support a categorical claim that this at-will covenant fails at inception.

The FTC's 2024 Non-Compete Clause Rule, 89 Fed. Reg. 38342 (May 7, 2024), was set aside nationwide and never took effect. *Ryan LLC v. FTC*, 739 F. Supp. 3d 496 (N.D. Tex. 2024). It supplies neither a ban nor a defense to California's separate statutes.

### Meridian's California exposure and the notice issue

Under §16600.5(e), Zhao may sue for injunctive relief, actual damages, or both; a prevailing employee in an action based on a violation is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees and costs. Actual damages require proof; the file supplies no amount. Section 925(c) separately permits a court to award reasonable fees to an employee enforcing his rights under that section, as well as injunctive and otherwise available relief. A Texas suit may thus create fee exposure even if Meridian ultimately obtains no restriction. The library supplies no supported dollar estimate of Zhao's damages or fees, and no basis to assert automatic damages or a statutory penalty amount.

Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16600.1(a) separately makes inclusion of a nonexcepted noncompete in an employment contract unlawful. Zhao signed on March 4, 2024, after that provision took effect. Subsection (c) treats a violation as an act of unfair competition. The **February 14, 2024 notice deadline** in §16600.1(b), however, covered then-current employees and former employees employed after January 1, 2022 with covered covenants. Zhao had not yet joined or signed on February 14; the record does not show that Meridian owed *him* that one-time notice. HR records say Meridian sent **no** California-employee notices. That creates a serious separate exposure for any employees or former employees in the statutory cohort with nonexcepted covenants, but their identities, contract terms, addresses, and count are absent. Do not describe the failure to notify Zhao by February 14 as a violation; audit the actual cohort promptly. Nothing in the provided §16600.1 text establishes a later individualized notice deadline for Zhao.

### Strongest arguments

**Meridian:** Texas has a substantial connection and Zhao agreed to an exclusive Texas forum. His technical role at a direct competitor could endanger confidential know-how and goodwill. If actual protected information was furnished, *Sheshunoff* may satisfy the ancillary requirement, and Texas's mandatory reformation rule offers a narrower injunction even if the written covenant is excessive. Zhao's September email has not clearly exercised §925's voiding right.

**Zhao:** He lived and worked almost entirely in California under a form agreement signed without negotiated counsel; his new job is there. Section 925 lets him void the Texas forum and law terms, while §187, *DeSantis*, and *Application Group* point to California law even in a Texas court. Sections 16600 and 16600.5 make this employment ban void and render an enforcement attempt actionable. The facially sweeping restriction would also exceed Texas's reasonableness requirement. Meridian has identified no taking, misuse, solicitation, or concrete threat beyond competitive employment.

## Recommendation

Do **not** file a Travis County §8 action or send a demand threatening its enforcement. Tell Zhao and Kestrel that Meridian will not seek to restrict lawful employment under §8, while reserving specific rights concerning actual confidential information and other independently valid obligations. Preserve access logs, devices, exit records, and proof of information supplied; assess any concrete misuse or solicitation separately before any claim. Do not use §8 as leverage in that process. Audit California agreements and the February 2024 notice cohort, then take California counsel's advice on remediation of the existing §16600.1 and §16600.5 exposure. Revise the employment form so it does not impose this noncompete on California employees. The unresolved facts could change a separate confidentiality or solicitation analysis, but they do not justify this §8 suit.
