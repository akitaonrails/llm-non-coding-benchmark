## 1. Schedule 3.3 is not "None" — three items must be listed

Your draft states there are no consent issues. The data room shows otherwise:

1. **Hensley Homes Master Supply Agreement (data room §1.1; 38% of revenue).** Section 18 prohibits assignment "by operation of law, merger, sale of assets, change of control or otherwise" without Hensley's prior written consent, which it "may ... withhold in its sole discretion," and provides that "any purported assignment in violation of this Section is void." A clause of exactly this type is enforced as written — transferring the agreement without consent is a breach and the transfer is void (*SQL Solutions v. Oracle*, 1991 WL 626458 (N.D. Cal.); Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§317, 322, per our reference set). This is a real closing item, not a formality. Our client will personally make the first approach to Hensley this week — before either party's counsel makes contact — and will pursue a signed consent before signing where possible; neither party should pretend the outcome is guaranteed while consent may be "withheld in sole discretion." Because that approach is underway, **we require a pre-closing customer-contact covenant** (proposed new §3.4 in our redline): neither Buyer nor its representatives may contact any customer of the Business regarding the transaction without Seller's prior written consent and participation. Buyer's diligence team visiting key customers before the consent conversation is finished is a deal risk, not a courtesy.
2. **Cascade Builders framework agreement (data room §1.2; 11% of revenue).** Assignment is permitted to a successor to substantially all assets **upon notice**. Your 3.1 transfers all assets of the Business, so the condition should be satisfied — but the notice must actually be given, so this belongs on Schedule 3.3 as a notice item.
3. **Shop lease, 7700 Burleson Rd (data room §3.1; lease §22).** Landlord consent required, not to be unreasonably withheld. We expect no difficulty but it is a required consent.

Please repopulate Schedule 3.3 accordingly. We will also require that the consents representation be qualified by the schedule — our client cannot sign a "no other consents required" rep that the data room itself contradicts. The consequences if Hensley declines (price adjustment vs. termination right, with an outside date) should be negotiated in the document now; we will not accept "commercially reasonable efforts" backed by a false schedule.

Related and on timing only: the City of Austin spray-booth permit (data room §4.1) is issued to the LLC and is non-transferable; the new owner must reapply (stated 30–60 days). We propose a covenant that Buyer file its reapplication before Closing so there is no operational gap; Seller will cooperate with the underlying compliance file.

## 2. WARN — confirmed, no notice obligation on Seller

Based on the data room census (data room §2.1–2.2): 14 W-2 employees (7 shop, 4 installers, 3 office) and 9 long-tenure installers currently paid on Form 1099. Even counting every worker as an employee, headcount is 23.

WARN applies to employers of **100 or more employees** (excluding part-time) and to plant closings or mass layoffs affecting 50+ employees at a single site (29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(1); 20 C.F.R. §639.3). Brightline is not a covered employer, and Texas has no mini-WARN statute. **No WARN notice is required of Seller**, including if Buyer elects not to hire every installer at Closing. Two cautions, both for your side: (i) our confirmation is as of today's census and addresses Seller's obligations only — Buyer's post-closing staffing decisions are Buyer's matter; and (ii) do not read this answer as agreement with anything in §4.12; headcount is below WARN thresholds *whatever* the classification answer is.

## 3. Our position on 7.4(c) (sandbagging) — and the section it multiplies

Your draft pairs three provisions: (a) a clean §4.12 classification rep; (b) a 7.4(b) carve-out raising §4.12 to a cap equal to the **entire Purchase Price**; and (c) a pro-sandbagging clause preserving Buyer's claims "regardless of knowledge ... before or after execution." You have the classification facts from the data room. Putting the three together, you are asking our client to indemnify you, up to the full price, for an issue you already know about, surviving to the statute of limitations, with escrow at 10%. That is not a "knowledge" tweak — it is the deal's largest risk transfer, and the characterization as market-standard is half right: pro-sandbagging is one of two standard positions, and it is the buyer-favorable one (ABA Private Target Deal Points Study practice materials in our reference set). A seller who discloses a known issue, gives a clean rep, and accepts pro-sandbagging has effectively agreed to indemnify for the disclosed issue.

Our position:

- **§4.12**: qualified by a disclosure schedule identifying the nine 1099 installers, the 2025 amounts, and their busy-season hours. Under the common-law control test (IRS Rev. Rul. 87-41; Tex. Lab. Code §201.041; 40 TAC §815.134), an unqualified rep would simply be false, and we will not negotiate as though it could be true.
- **§7.4(b)**: §4.12 comes back under the 10% Cap. Buyer's real concern is pre-closing worker-classification exposure for the nine — employment taxes and, as you now know from the corrected schedule, their busy-season hours (45–50 per week, paid straight hourly), which carry FLSA overtime exposure. We will cover both directly with a **special indemnity capped at $150,000**, escrow-backed, exclusive absent fraud. That cap is sized, not pulled from the air: reduced-rate federal employment tax on the 2025 payments runs ≈ $18,543.60/year (1.5% × $612,000 + 20% × 7.65% × $612,000), ≈ $74,000 over the open years; the overtime shortfall computes to roughly $10,000–$34,000/year across the nine (half-time premium of $18.75/hour on 60–200 overtime hours per installer). $150,000 covers the credible combined range — and note that in a substantial-continuity asset sale your client may itself face the FLSA wage claims as successor (our reference set), so a full-price cap asks it to be covered twice for the same dollars. This is more credible protection than a full-price cap on a rep both sides know is breached: no one benefits from a court asked to reform or police an unconscionable carve-out, and the 10% general cap plus a sized special indemnity is the market architecture.
- **§7.4(c)**: with the schedule exception in place, we can live with a knowledge clause; **without it, 7.4(c) comes out** and is replaced by an anti-sandbagging formulation (no recovery for breaches Buyer actually knew of at signing, including data-room disclosures). Pick one of the recognized seller protections — schedule exception, anti-sandbagging, or capped special indemnity. A clean rep plus pro-sandbagging plus a full-price cap is the one combination we will decline.

We note your two-week signing target is workable **only if** the Hensley consent approach starts now; a consent that may be withheld in sole discretion cannot be compressed into a signing calendar after the fact. We propose Seller circulates the corrected Schedule 3.3 and a revised §4.12/7.4 package within 3 business days, and Seller contacts Hensley this week.
