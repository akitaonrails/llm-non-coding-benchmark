# Note to Ray Brightline — what could cost you money after closing

Ray — you asked what bites you later. Three things, in order of how much they can cost you. Numbers shown so you can check them. (One assumption: the draft doesn't state the price; the escrow says "$310,000 (10%)", so the price reads as **$310,000 ÷ 0.10 = $3,100,000**. Tell me if that's wrong — several numbers below move with it.)

## 1. The nine "1099 installers" — the buyer already knows, and the draft makes it YOUR problem, uncapped

They work only for you, show up on the foreman's daily schedule, drive your trucks, use your tools, get paid hourly ($34–41/hr), and average 3.4 years each. Under the IRS's control test that's employment — "everyone in the industry does it" is not a defense, and the data room shows these facts to the buyer in black and white. 2025 payments to them: $612,000 (about $68,000 each).

Now look at what the buyer's draft does with that:

- §4.12 asks you to *represent* everyone is properly classified. You'd be signing a false statement on day one.
- §7.4(b) says breaches of that one rep are capped not at the usual 10% but at the **whole price — $3,100,000** — and the survival runs to the tax statute of limitations, years past closing.
- §7.4(c) (the "knowledge" clause) says the buyer can claim **even for problems it already knew about**. The buyer has your data room. You disclose the issue, then indemnify them for it, uncapped.

Rough size of the actual problem (federal, using the reduced non-willful rates in IRC §3509 — the library cites the statute but not its percentages, so treat the rates as estimates): 1.5% × $612,000 = $9,180, plus 20% of employer FICA (7.65% × $612,000 = $46,818) = $9,364 — about **$18,500/yr**, so call it **$75,000–$80,000 for four open years**.

And now that you've told me they work **45–50 hours a week in busy season for straight hourly pay**, there is a second number — overtime. If they're employees (that's the premise of this whole section), hours over 40 must be paid at 1.5×. You already paid them the straight part, so the gap is the half-time premium: average rate ($34 + $41) ÷ 2 = $37.50, half of that = **$18.75 per overtime hour**. Low case 5 overtime hours × 12 busy weeks = $1,125/installer/yr; high case 10 hours × 20 weeks = $3,750/installer/yr. Across nine installers that's **$10,125–$33,750/yr**, or **$40,500–$135,000 over four open years**. (I'm assuming busy season runs 12–20 weeks — tell me the real number and I'll fix it.) There are penalties that can double wage claims and stretch the lookback to three years if a violation is called willful — those rules are outside my verified reference set, so treat them as possible multipliers, not givens. "They like it" does not help: employee preference is not something you can sign away when the government or a lawyer recomputes the pay stubs.

Put it together: taxes ≈ $75k + overtime $40k–$135k ≈ **$115,000–$210,000** of realistic exposure, before state unemployment. That is why the number below moved.

**The fix (already drafted in the redline):** put the nine AND their busy-season hours on a disclosure schedule instead of lying in the rep, and offer a **special indemnity capped at $150,000** (go no higher than **$200,000** if that's what it takes to close), held in the escrow, as the buyer's only remedy for this. Compare: the draft makes this a **$3,100,000** problem. The library-backed market rule is blunt: carving one operational rep like worker classification up to a full-price cap is off-market and should be resisted. Say no to the full-price carve-out, no to the clean rep, and no to the pro-knowledge clause unless the schedule fix is in.

## 2. Hensley Homes — 38% of your revenue, and their contract says the sale is void without their signature

Hensley is your biggest customer: 38% of $4.6M = **$1,748,000/yr**. Their Master Supply Agreement, §18, says you can't assign it "by ... sale of assets" without their written consent, they can say no "in its sole discretion", and a transfer without consent is **void**. The buyer's Schedule 3.3 says "None" — no consents needed. That's wrong, and signing that page plus the rep behind it means you personally start the deal in breach.

You've now told me the part that decides how this whole section plays out: the Hensley owner is your friend, **you haven't told him you're selling**, and he "won't love a corporate buyer." That makes sequencing the most valuable thing you own right now, and it works like this:

1. **You tell him first — personally, this week.** Not a letter, not me, not the buyer. You, in person, before anything is signed. A friend hearing about his cabinet supplier being sold to "Lone Star Millwork Holdings LLC" from a buyer's diligence call is how a yes turns into a sole-discretion no.
2. **Frame the buyer the way you actually believe it** — continuity, same shop, same crews — and ask for his blessing before the paperwork arrives. If the personal conversation goes well, the formal consent request becomes a formality, and we want the **signed consent in hand before you sign the APA**. That converts the deal's biggest condition into a non-event.
3. **Until that conversation has happened, nobody on the buyer's side talks to Hensley about the sale.** I've put a customer-contact covenant into the redline for exactly this. Same discipline for Cascade Builders (11% = $506,000/yr — only *notice* required) and the landlord (consent, "not unreasonably withheld").
4. **If he reacts badly**, you are looking at $1,748,000/yr of revenue walking, and the buyer will demand a price cut or walk itself. That is why the contract now has to answer the "what if Hensley says no" question up front — defined price cut or termination right with an outside date — instead of leaving you at the mercy of a veto after you've already told your friend his supplier is changing hands.

This is still a process problem, not a deal-killer — but only if you make the call this week.

## 3. The earn-out is nearly worth zero as written — don't trade real money for it

The draft pays 40% of next-year EBITDA over $700,000, max $400,000. Do the math:

- To collect the full $400,000, EBITDA must hit $700,000 + $400,000 ÷ 0.40 = **$1,700,000** — that's **129.7% above** your reported $740,000. Nobody doubles EBITDA in a year under new owners.
- At your current run-rate, the payout is 40% × ($740,000 − $700,000) = **$16,000**. At best.
- Worse: your $740,000 counts on add-backs (your $180,000 salary + $42,000 legal). Under *buyer's* accounting policies — which is what the draft says EBITDA means — that's $740,000 − $222,000 = **$518,000**, which is **$182,000 under the threshold**: the earn-out pays **$0**, and the buyer controls the calculator. This exact trick (buyer allocates overhead and integration costs into "EBITDA as determined by Buyer") is the known pathology in the case law.

**The fix:** a real EBITDA definition (your historical practices, buyer overhead and integration costs excluded), ordinary-course operation, audit rights, an outside accountant judging disputes, acceleration if they flip the business — or, simpler, treat the earn-out as worth ~nothing and demand more cash at closing.

## Whitaker says it's "market standard," and you ask: can we just sign?

Short answer: **not the draft in front of you. With three fixes, yes — and two weeks is still doable.**

Whitaker's two lines are each half right:

- **"7.4(c) is market, every deal has it."** Half right. That clause comes in two flavors — buyer-favorable and seller-favorable — and both exist in real deals (my reference set carries both). "Every deal" is a sales pitch. The buyer-favorable version is tolerable when the reps are honest; it's poison paired with a rep you know is false.
- **"4.12 is plain-vanilla — if the contractors are fine, what's the concern?"** The words are plain-vanilla; the facts aren't. Under the IRS test your installers are employees — exclusive work, your foreman's daily schedule, your trucks and tools, hourly, 3.4 years. Here's the joke in his sentence, though: **if they were really "fine," the disclosure schedule and a $150,000 capped indemnity would cost the buyer nothing** — nothing to claim. Him demanding $3,100,000 of coverage for a risk he calls a non-issue tells you what he actually thinks. My reply puts that test to him in writing: accept the cap if you believe your own sentence.

What signing *this* draft actually means:

1. You sign Schedule 3.3 saying "None" — false the day you sign (Hensley, Cascade notice, landlord).
2. You sign §4.12 saying everyone is properly classified — false the day you sign.
3. You sign 7.4(b) + 7.4(c), which price that false statement at **the whole $3,100,000**, surviving years past the $310,000 escrow — for a problem the buyer already has in its own files.

The three fixes that make this signable: put the nine (and their hours) on a Schedule 4.12 disclosure; cap the classification indemnity at **$150,000–$200,000, held in escrow**; fill Schedule 3.3 honestly. With those, Whitaker can keep his sandbagging clause and most of his draft, and you sign on schedule — provided the Hensley conversation starts this week. Don't argue clause philosophy with Whitaker; put the three fixes on the table and make him price his own "no concern."

## Two smaller things that only bite if you're careless

- **"I'll sign whatever non-compete."** Don't. Seven years / 500 miles is more than a court would enforce for a shop like yours (Texas law: 3–5 years in your trade area is the typical enforceable range for a business sale, and a court will trim it, not delete it — meaning you'd still be bound). We've countered at 3 years, Travis County plus neighbors. If you ever consult, help a friend's shop, or hate retirement in year two, that page matters.
- **Small claims tail** (Hardaway $9,800 + warranty log $23,000 = $32,800): under the $75,000 deductible basket you'd pay nothing unless total claims cross $75,000 — keep the basket deductible and disclose these openly.
