## 1. Stop collecting today — and here's why

Put in writing to the AR team that the standing instruction to keep collecting is over; route anything Redwood-related to counsel.

**Why:** Redwood filed Chapter 11 on **March 10, 2026**. The filing triggers the automatic stay, which prohibits *any act to collect, assess, or recover a pre-petition claim* — 11 U.S.C. §362(a)(6). Our library is explicit that dunning letters, collection calls, and threats to report to credit bureaus after the petition are prohibited acts. Mesa has already done all three:

- **Mar 18** — email to the CFO threatening to hold shipments and report to credit bureaus. This one is the worst: it went out *after* Redwood's AP told us on 3/15 that it had filed.

**"It's automated" makes it worse, not better.** Frank says the FINAL NOTICE letters go out automatically to anyone 30 days late. Two problems:

1. **The program is presumably still running.** Every further automated letter or email to Redwood is a *fresh* stay violation. **Today, someone with authority must pull the Redwood account out of the dunning cycle** and confirm in writing that nothing else is scheduled — including credit-bureau reporting.
2. Automation is not an excuse — it's a pattern. Once Mesa knew of the filing (no later than the 3/15 call), continuing automated notices reads as reckless-to-willful. A creditor who *willfully* violates the stay pays the debtor's **actual damages, costs and attorneys' fees, and in appropriate cases punitive damages** — §362(k) — and courts also sanction creditors under §105 (our library flags this; the §105 point is practice, not statutory text in the library). A short corrective note from counsel to debtor's counsel (stop-the-train, no further demands) is the standard way to cap this risk [practice recommendation]. Also: preserve the emails, call logs, and program settings now, and tell Frank, Lori, and the credit dept — in writing — that all Redwood contact goes through counsel.

## 2. What Mesa may *owe* Redwood

Filing claims is only half the picture. Redwood's estate (or the DIP lender funding it) will look at everything Mesa was paid in the **90 days before March 10** — the "preference" window (§547(b), insolvency presumed in the window per §547(f)). Mesa received **$180,000** in that window:

| Date | Amount | Days after invoice | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dec 18, 2025 | $51,000 | 45 | ACH |
| Jan 22, 2026 | $44,000 | 43 | ACH |
| Feb 10, 2026 | $48,200 | **29** | ACH |
| Feb 27, 2026 | $36,800 | **25** | **WIRE, demanded by our credit manager** |

Our defenses (we bear the burden, §547(g)):

- **Ordinary course (§547(c)(2)):** the Dec/Jan payments match our 12-month history of 43–47-day ACH payments — likely protected ($95,000). The **Feb 10 payment (29 days, after our own unilateral NET-15 terms change)** and the **Feb 27 wire-on-demand** look *abnormal* against our history — the library says shortened terms, pressure, and wire-on-demand generally fall outside the defense ($85,000 exposed). The industry-norms fallback (§547(c)(2)(B)) **is currently unavailable: Frank confirms Mesa has no data on normal payment terms in the steel trade.** So the 2/10 and 2/27 payments must be saved, if at all, by the new-value defense below.
- **New value (§547(c)(4)):** after each of the two risky payments we shipped more steel on unsecured, unpaid terms: $48,200 of goods+freight after Feb 10, and $51,200 (goods + services) after Feb 27. That offsets the exposure almost dollar-for-dollar. **[Label: treating freight and services as "new value" is standard doctrine; the library text doesn't spell it out — flag as unverified but expected.]**
- **Bottom line:** realistic clawback exposure is near **$0** (worst realistic case ~$6,900; absolute worst case ~$128,800 if *every* payment is avoided and new value is disallowed). But do not spend this money — see next point.

**The trap to know (§502(d)): if a creditor is liable for a preference, the court *shall disallow its claim* until it pays the preference back.** So preference exposure isn't just a cash claim against us — it can freeze Mesa's entire distribution and its vote. If a preference demand comes, negotiate/resolve it promptly or the $125,000 claim sits.

## 2b. "WE OWE THEM?! They owe us $210,000 and we might have to give back money for steel we delivered?" — Frank's objection, answered head-on

Nobody is saying Mesa did anything wrong, and nobody is taking back money for steel that stays unpaid. Here is what preference law actually does, in plain English:

**1. It's about timing and equal treatment, not wrongdoing.** When Redwood filed, the law froze the last **90 days** of payments (Dec 10 → Mar 10) and asks: "did one creditor get paid while others got letters?" Anything paid in that window — **$180,000 to us** — is *reviewed*, because Redwood was presumed insolvent then (§547(f)). Reviewing is not clawing back. The clawback only survives if our defenses fail.

**2. Our defenses are strong on most of it — because of our own records.** The Dec 18 ($51,000) and Jan 22 ($44,000) payments came 45 and 43 days after invoice, by ACH, on the same monthly rhythm as the eight payments before them (43–47 days, all ACH). That is textbook "ordinary course of business" (§547(c)(2)(A)) — **$95,000 almost certainly stays where it is.**

**3. The two payments at risk are the ones *we* made unusual.** The Feb 10 payment arrived in 29 days and the Feb 27 payment in 25 days **by wire we demanded** — right after *our* credit dept rewrote terms to NET 15 on Jan 20. That's *abnormal* compared with the parties' own history, and the library says shortened terms, pressure, and wire-on-demand generally fall outside the ordinary-course defense. That $85,000 is the theoretical exposure — created by our own conduct in January–February, not by anything unfair about accepting a payment.

**4. And the knockout for even that $85,000: we kept shipping on credit after each of those payments.** After Feb 10 we delivered $48,200 more (2/20 structural steel + 2/26 freight) and after Feb 27 another $51,200 (3/4 coil + 3/9 cutting) — all unsecured, none of it paid. Unpaid "new value" offsets preference exposure dollar-for-dollar (§547(c)(4)). Run the math: $85,000 exposed, ~$99,400 and $51,200 of unpaid deliveries standing behind the two payments. **Realistic clawback: about $0 (worst realistic ~$6,900 if freight doesn't count; the scary $128,800 number assumes every defense fails — the records don't support that).** So the honest answer to "can't be right": right to be annoyed — but with these records, Mesa keeps the money. [Caveat, labeled: the burden of proving the defenses is ours (§547(g)), and the new-value offset is strong but not automatic.]

**5. Even in the bad movie, we don't pay for free steel.** If a court ever did "avoid" a payment, the money returns to the estate *and the corresponding claim revives* — it swaps a cash payment for an unsecured claim, not a confiscation. [Label: claim-reinstatement is standard doctrine; not spelled out in our library — verify with counsel.] In practice these things are usually netted against the creditor's claim in settlement [practice].

**6. Why we can't just ignore it.** Two reasons. (a) §502(d): if exposure exists and isn't resolved, the court *shall disallow* Mesa's whole claim until it's paid — the tail wags the dog. (b) The DIP lender writing Redwood's checks runs exactly this analysis on every vendor; the number will surface whether we name it or not. Better to walk into the critical-vendor negotiation already owning a defensible position than to be blindsided. Holding the $85,000 quiet in the account costs nothing and keeps the settlement option open.

**7. About "they owe us $210,000."** Every memo and the claim draft say $125,000 because that is the *only* number Mesa's own invoice ledger proves — five open invoices totaling $125,000, while the automated letter said $210,000. Frank asserting $210,000 again doesn't move the number; **invoices do.** If accounting can produce the missing invoices/aging detail before the June 11 bar date, the proof of claim is amendable upward and the 503(b)(9)/GUC split gets recomputed from delivery dates. Until then we file what we can prove — an unprovable claim invites an objection and makes everything else we assert look soft. And note the arithmetic that cuts the other way: if the estate ever avoids payments, the claim goes *up*, not down — $210,000 of open invoices is worth more to us as a claim we can actually document.

## 3. Why "file something and get paid first" is mostly wrong — and what actually works

- **Filing a claim does not create priority.** A proof of claim just preserves Mesa's right to share. The CEO's instinct to "file something" is right only in the sense that the **bar date is June 11, 2026** — miss it and the claim is disallowed absent excusable neglect (a hard standard). File Form 410 for **$125,000** well before then. Draft is ready (`proof_of_claim_draft.md`).
- **The real priority win: §503(b)(9).** Goods we delivered in the 20 days before the petition (structural steel MS-7788, coil MS-7815 — **$78,100**) are paid as **administrative expenses**, ahead of general unsecured claims. That's 62% of our claim upgraded, and it doesn't need anyone's permission — just proper assertion (filed separately, typically by motion per the bar-date order). The freight and cutting-services pieces ($21,300) do **not** qualify — statute excludes them.
- **The legitimate path to early payment of *pre-petition* dollars is critical-vendor status — and Redwood has already put it on the table.** Redwood's counsel asked Mesa whether it would **keep supplying on normal terms if the old invoices are paid**. The CEO answered **"pay first."** That instinct is actually correct — and there is a pending motion built exactly for it: **Dkt. 44, the critical-vendor motion, hearing April 2, 2026.** Under *Kmart*, the debtor can ask the court to **pay a vendor's pre-petition claims up front** in exchange for that vendor **continuing to supply on customary trade terms during the case**. "Pay first, then we keep supplying" is precisely the deal a critical-vendor order consummates — but it only happens through the debtor's motion and the court's approval, not through side assurances. The CEO's reply (made without knowing the motion existed) left the deal hanging; if no vendor commits for the hearing, the motion may pass Mesa by. **[Action: before April 2, have Mesa's counsel call Redwood's counsel and say: Mesa is prepared to continue supply on customary trade terms, conditioned on payment of the pre-petition claim under the Dkt. 44 order.]** Whether the court approves, and whether Redwood even lists Mesa as critical, is the debtor's call and the court's call — Mesa cannot force it. [Note: refusing to deal post-petition is not by itself a stay violation, and conditioning new shipments on old money is a commercial position, not a legal one — but the library doesn't address this, so treat it as unverified; the safe course is to run the whole negotiation through the motion.]
- **Reclamation: don't chase it.** We could still serve a written §546(c) demand by about **March 30** for steel Redwood received Feb 4–Mar 4, but the statute subordinates it to prior liens, and the library notes the DIP lender holds a blanket lien — so it would recover nothing. Default decision: **do not serve** (no client go-ahead, and identifiability of the goods is unknown); bargaining chip only.
- **Do not "trade" the stay violations.** If Redwood raises the (at least) three stay violations — including whatever further automated notices go out before the program is switched off — the sensible posture is a prompt corrective letter and a negotiated standstill on that issue, not escalation.
