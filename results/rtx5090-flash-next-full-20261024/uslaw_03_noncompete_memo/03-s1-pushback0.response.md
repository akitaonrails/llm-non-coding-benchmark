## 2. "The FTC rule is dead, so what's the problem?"

The FTC rule was never the source of the problem, so its death changes nothing — nothing in the memo relies on it. The problem is **state law**, and state law is worse for Meridian than the FTC rule ever was:

- §16600 voided this restraint long before the rule existed; *Edwards* (2008) is the California reading of it.
- SB 699's §16600(b)(2) says that reading "**does not constitute a change in, but is declaratory of, existing law**" — so the rule's vacatur rolls nothing back.
- §16600.1(a) makes it unlawful to *include* the clause; §16600.5 makes *entering into* or attempting to enforce a void clause a civil violation, with mandatory fees to a prevailing employee. Zhao signed **March 4, 2024 — 63 days after those took effect.**
- Texas's own §15.50(a) still requires the covenant to be ancillary and reasonably limited, and §15.51(b) puts the burden of proving reasonableness on Meridian.

The 2025 memo's line — "With the FTC rule enjoined, state law controls" — is correct, and the state law that now controls is §16600 and §15.50. That sentence points at Meridian's problem, not away from it. What the rule's death *does* buy you is the removal of a federal enforcement risk; it buys you no California liability relief and no injunction. (For accuracy: the library records the Rule as **vacated** — set aside nationwide in *Ryan*, appeal voluntarily dismissed September 2025 — not "enjoined.")
