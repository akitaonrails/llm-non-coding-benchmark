**Privileged & confidential — prepared March 19, 2026.** Plain-English summary; every legal

## The short answer to "file something and get paid first"

Filing by itself does not put anyone first. What actually moves Mesa up the payment line is:

1. **A §503(b)(9) administrative claim for $78,100** — goods Redwood received in the 20 days
   before it filed (invoices MS-7788 and MS-7815). The library makes that claim an
   *administrative expense*; that administrative expenses are paid ahead of general unsecured
   creditors is standard bankruptcy practice but is **not restated in the library** — treated
   as unverified and to be confirmed against the plan's priority terms.
2. **Critical-vendor treatment** (below) — the only legitimate route to being paid *early* for
   the *pre-petition* balance. A motion is already pending in the case (Dkt. 44) and the
   hearing is **April 2, 2026**.
3. A **proof of claim filed by June 11, 2026** — this protects the vote and the dividend but
   pays nothing by itself.

What we must **not** do is keep collecting while we file. That converts a $125,000 receivable
into a damages lawsuit against Mesa (next section).

## 1. Stop doing this today — and why

**Every collection act against Redwood is now illegal.** Redwood filed Chapter 11 on March 10,
2026. The automatic stay bars *any act to collect, assess, or recover a pre-petition claim*,
and dunning letters, collection calls and threats to report to credit bureaus count as
collection. Four things already broke the stay — three documented in the AR log, plus one
phone call the client recalled:

| Date | What Mesa did | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Mar 12 | "FINAL NOTICE — pay $210,000 in 5 days or we refer to collections" (mail + email) | Act to collect a pre-petition claim — and note, $85,000 of that $210,000 had **already been paid** (see §6) |
| Mar 15 | Call to Redwood AP; our clerk said "that's not our problem, you owe us" after being told the debtor filed | Collection attempt; from this call **Mesa had actual knowledge of the case** |
| Mar 18 | Email to CFO threatening to hold shipments and **report to credit bureaus** | Threats to report to credit bureaus are collection acts |
| [date unknown] | When Redwood's counsel asked whether we'd keep supplying on normal terms, Frank answered **"pay first"** | Conditioning new steel on old money is a likely collection act (not squarely addressed in our library — flagged unverified) and it **kills the critical-vendor request** (§4 below). We need the date of this call. |

A **willful** stay violation is expensive. The stay statute makes an injured party recover actual
damages **plus costs and attorneys' fees**, and punitive damages in appropriate cases; the
statute's damages section speaks of an injured "individual," but our library records that courts
**also sanction creditors for willful violations against corporate debtors (under §105)** — the
practical exposure is the same. Because
our staff was told on March 15 that Redwood filed,
anything after that date is easy to characterize as willful — which is why pinning down the
date of Frank's "pay first" call matters. Damages and fee awards can rival the receivable
itself, and a stay-violation judgment makes every other negotiating position worse.

**Directives effective immediately (in writing to AR/credit staff):**
- No letters, calls, e-mails, statements, credit-hold threats, credit-bureau reports or
  "referral to collections" concerning Redwood.
- **No more shipping-for-payment deals.** Lori's "no steel until the wire hits our account"
  and Frank's "pay first" must not be repeated. If we ship, we ship on ordinary case terms;
  if we want payment for old invoices, that happens through the critical-vendor motion or a
  negotiated deal with the estate — not at the counter.
- Route every Redwood communication through counsel.
- Do not offset or withhold anything against Redwood without court relief — setoff of
  pre-petition mutual debts requires relief from the stay.
- Preserve all documents (AR notes, e-mails, shipping and delivery records).

## 2. What Mesa may owe Redwood (yes, owed — to the estate)

The Chapter 11 estate can sue Mesa for **preferences**: payments we received in the 90 days
before filing (window opens Dec 10, 2025) — the estate does not even have to prove Redwood was
insolvent then; the statute presumes it during the 90 days. We took in **$180,000** in that window:

- $51,000 (Dec 18) and $44,000 (Jan 22) — paid 45/43 days after invoice by ACH, continuing the
  parties' pre-window pattern (the six earlier payments, Apr–Nov 2025, were all 43–47 days after
  invoice, always by ACH). Defensible as ordinary course →
  likely protected. (The Jan 22 payment came two days after our Jan 20 terms-change e-mail,
  but its speed and method were unchanged, so it stays in the protected column.)
- $48,200 (Feb 10) — paid in 29 days after we unilaterally cut terms from net 45 to net 15 on
  Jan 20 **and made every new shipment conditional on paying the oldest open invoice** (Lori
  confirms). Shortened terms, pressure and divergent timing fall outside the ordinary-course
  protection.
- $36,800 (Feb 27) — a **partial payment by wire we demanded**; Lori confirms she told them
  "no steel until it hit our account." Demanded wires and partials are classic preference
  targets.

Gross exposure therefore reaches **$85,000** — our own admissions (shortened terms, shipping
pressure, a demanded wire) match exactly the factors that break the ordinary-course defense, so
realistically it is no longer available for those two payments. The defense is **new value**,
and under the statute in our materials it is **not limited to steel**: after the Feb 10 payment
Mesa gave **$99,400 of unpaid new value** ($78,100 goods + $6,900 freight + $14,400 cutting
services), and after the Feb 27 payment it gave **$51,200 more** ($36,800 steel + $14,400
services). Nothing was paid after Feb 27, so nothing cuts that credit back. On steel alone it
still works — $78,100 followed Feb 10 and exactly $36,800 followed Feb 27 — so the realistic
**net exposure is about $0**. Worst case ~$36,800 if a court holds that new value given after
the *last* payment cannot be credited against that last payment; that sequencing rule is not in
the materials we have and is flagged as unverified.

Two estate levers matter more than the exposure math:
- **§502(d): until a creditor pays back what it owes as a preference, the court must disallow
  that creditor's claim.** Even a contested preference argument can freeze our entire $125,000
  claim. Plan to resolve preference issues with the estate before distribution.
- The debtor can bring preference claims and stay-violation claims against each other —
  our documented violations (plus the undated "pay first" call) are the estate's
  counter-weapon. Stop the conduct now so
  the damages number stays at "three documented incidents" instead of growing weekly.

## 3. Reclamation — assert it, but don't build on it

We can still make a **written reclamation demand by March 30, 2026** for goods received in the
45 days before filing (MS-7740 balance, MS-7788, MS-7815 — $103,700 unpaid value). It is cheap
to do and preserves leverage — but the statute subordinates reclamation to **prior liens, and the library note for this case
records the DIP lender's blanket lien** (the DIP order itself is not in our file — scope
confirmed as an open unknown, U2 in `QUESTIONS.md`), so in practice reclaiming actual steel
is unlikely to yield anything. Treat it as leverage for the critical-vendor negotiation, not as
a payment plan.

## 4. The critical-vendor path — the real answer to "get paid first"

A debtor may ask the court for authority to pay pre-petition claims of vendors whose goods are
essential and who **cannot readily be replaced**; the vendor typically must agree to **customary
trade terms for the duration of the case**. Our materials (a practice note citing *In re Kmart
Corp.*, 7th Cir. 2004) confine this relief to exactly that situation — it is the legitimate
route to early payment of pre-petition claims, at the debtor's discretion and subject to court
approval, not something a creditor can obtain by filing papers on its own.

**The opening — and how we nearly lost it:** Redwood's counsel has already asked whether we'd
keep supplying on normal terms, and Frank answered **"pay first."** That is the wrong answer
twice over: a critical-vendor designation requires the vendor to agree to **customary trade
terms for the case duration**, and conditioning new steel on old money is exactly the reason a
debtor (or the court) says no. Frank also did not know a motion is pending (**Dkt. 44, hearing
April 2, 2026**) — the court notice says creditors wishing to be considered **must contact
debtor's counsel**.

**Facts still in our favor:** the file shows a steady supply relationship — eight invoice-and-
payment cycles from Apr 2025 through Feb 2026 (roughly monthly, with gaps in June and Oct 2025),
and our on-site cutting service (invoice MS-7819) is documented. The rest of the essentiality
case — whether Redwood can source these grades elsewhere, lead times, substitute suppliers — is
**not evidenced in the file** and must be built from Mesa's sales/purchasing records before
Apr 2.

**Course correction before April 2 (this week):** (1) contact Redwood's counsel, retract the
"pay first" answer, and put a written commitment on the table to keep supplying on ordinary
case terms; (2) build the essentiality showing; (3) pair the ask with the §503(b)(9) priority
claim so the total package looks like value to the estate, not old-debt relief; and
(4) show good conduct — the stay violations in §1 are exactly what a debtor cites when it
refuses to designate a vendor "critical."
