## 1. Your 1099 installers — the indemnity cap is the whole fight

Nine installers are 1099 contractors who "work scheduled weekly installs," and the file says they
"may meet employee classification tests." They did the same work your W-2 crew does, at about
$68,000 each per year.

Why it bites: you will sign §4.12 as a **clean** promise that every worker was properly classified.
If the IRS ever decides they were employees, that clean promise is broken — and in the buyer's
draft, worker-classification claims are carved out of the 10% cap. So you would pay up to the
**entire sale price**, not just the escrow.

Show the arithmetic:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Escrow (§9.3, "10%" of price) | $310,000 |
| Normal cap (§7.4(b), 10% of price) | $310,000 |
| Cap on a §4.12 breach, buyer's draft (§7.4(c)) | $3,100,000 (100% of price) |
| Money exposed **beyond** your escrow | $3,100,000 − $310,000 = **$2,790,000** |

The tax bill itself is illustrative only — the rates are not in our reference file, so treat these
as planning numbers, not advice you can rely on: employer FICA ≈ 9 × $68,000 × 7.65% ≈ $140,454;
FUTA 9 × $7,000 × 0.6% = $378/yr; unpaid overtime, if they were on site ~45 hrs/week, ≈ $42,188/yr;
over three years ≈ **$268,150**, before penalties and interest.

One number in the file does not reconcile, and we are not forcing it: $68,000 ÷ the $34–41/hr range
implies 1,659–2,000 hours per year (about 33–40 hrs/week). A 40-hour week at the $37.50 midpoint
already pays $75,000, which is more than $68,000. So the hourly rate and the annual pay cannot both
be right unless the hours were under 40/week — and nobody has told us the hours. Overtime exposure
could be $0 or could be six figures.

**What we are doing about it:** we told the buyer we cannot certify §4.12 clean, and we put the
installers on an exception schedule. If the buyer refuses an exception (his draft defines 3.3 as
"no exceptions"), our fallback is a sized special indemnity — a fixed number, escrow-only — never
the 100%-of-price carve-out. We will not sign §7.4(c) as drafted. Do not let anyone talk you into
it as "standard."

## 2. The Hensley contract can transfer void — and it is 38% of your revenue

Your largest account — Hensley Property Group, multi-site Dallas–Fort Worth residential builds,
**38% of Brightline's revenue** — says Brightline "may not assign without Lessor's sole discretion
consent," and that an assignment without consent is **void**. "Void" is the worst word in contract
law: a consent-conditions clause you blow through in an asset deal does not just breach the
contract, it means the contract never moved to the buyer.

Show the arithmetic:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue share | 38% × $4,600,000 = $1,748,000/yr |
| EBITDA share (at the $740k adjusted figure) | 38% × $740,000 = $281,200/yr |
| Value at the deal's own multiple (5.98 × unadjusted EBITDA) | 38% × $518,000 × 5.98 ≈ **$824,600** |
| Your general cap if the buyer sues over it (§7.4(b)) | $310,000 |
| Your shortfall if the buyer prices it in | ≈ $514,600, **plus** the earn-out |

And the earn-out: if Hensley walks, the best remaining 2026 EBITDA is about $740,000 × 0.62 =
$543,160, which is below the $700,000 floor — so you would earn **$0** on top of everything else.

The buyer's draft makes this *your* risk twice over: §3.3 as drafted makes you affirmatively promise
that no key contract needs consent to close (Hensley does), and §2.4 lists "customer consents" as a
closing item — meaning if Hensley has not signed a consent by the closing date, **you** are the
party in breach of the APA.

**What we are doing about it:** we scheduled Hensley and every other contract that needs consent,
we are asking the buyer to pick a realistic closing date (30–60 days after Hensley consents, not a
fixed date), and we asked you for the two facts we need: did you get the 2017 consent, and **is the
lease personally guaranteed by you** (see Q5 — if it is, the Hensley fight is your personal fight,
not just the deal's).

## 3. The earn-out is mostly decoration — the flat $16,000 is the safe money

The buyer offered 40% of 2026 EBITDA over $700,000, capped at $400,000. Against your adjusted 2025
EBITDA of $740,000, that looks like a real bonus. It mostly isn't, for three reasons:

- **It ignores your own add-backs.** The buyer added back $180,000 of your salary and $42,000 of
  legal fees to get $740,000. He did not say the earn-out uses that same math. On the unadjusted
  $518,000, 2026 pays **$0** unless 2026 is ~$182,000 better than 2025.
- **The cap is nearly unreachable.** To get the full $400,000 you need 2026 EBITDA of
  ($400,000 ÷ 0.40) + $700,000 = **$1,700,000** — more than double 2025. The formula maxes out; it
  never pays more than $400,000, so the upside beyond $1.7M is worth nothing to you.
- **The buyer runs the shop for 12 months.** He computes the number, he keeps the cabins business,
  he controls the spend. And if Hensley is lost at closing, the earn-out is $0 before he even tries.

Realistic outlook: a flat $740,000 2026 pays (0.40 × $40,000) = **$16,000**. So plan on ~$16,000,
not $400,000 — and we are asking in the redline for the earn-out to use the same adjusted-EBITDA
definition, with an audit right and a duty to operate in the ordinary course.

## Two more things to know, because your two-week timeline is at risk

- **The consent problem above is the timeline problem.** "Sign in two weeks" only works if you
  actually *close* in two weeks. If Hensley consent (or the Austin permit, or the 45-day Lessor
  notice for the leased shop) is a closing item, the signing date is not the finish line — you just
  start a clock you may not control. Q7 asks whether the City of Austin lets the buyer run the
  showroom before a use permit is issued; nobody in the file answers it. If not, closing waits for
  the permit.
- **Do not sign anything that says "no known Real or Potential Real Rep breaches."** The data room
  itself says "no pending claims" while the file also contains the Hardaway lien ($9,800) and about
  $23,000 of open warranty repair quotes. Those are cheap problems — under the $75,000 deductible —
  but a blanket "all clean" rep is how a $32,800 mess turns into an indemnity claim. They go on
  schedules; that is what schedules are for.
