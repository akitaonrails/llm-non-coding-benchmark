## Why a bigger check can leave you with less

**One sentence:** Lone Star pays you $250,000 more but their structure costs you $437,200 more in tax — so you end up **$187,200 behind**.

**The real reason — the two buyers buy different things:**

- **Hensley buys your *company***. To the IRS that's one simple sale of something you've owned for years: one tax rate, 20%. You keep **80 of every $100**.
- **Lone Star buys your *stuff*, item by item** — and when the IRS looks inside each item, it remembers history. Three expensive memories:

1. **The equipment.** Over the years you deducted $860,000 of depreciation — that saved you 37¢ per dollar. When you sell the equipment above its written-down book value, the IRS *takes that favor back at 37%*, not 20%. Lone Star's own paperwork puts $900,000 on equipment, making $660,000 of your price "payback" → **+$112,200** of extra tax versus 20%.
2. **The $400,000 "non-compete payment."** A check labeled *"we're paying you not to reopen the shop next door"* is compensation, not a business sale — 37%, not 20%. Same money, different label, **+$68,000**.
3. **Your C-corp past.** Your LLC was a C-corporation until 2023. If its old gains get sold **before 1/1/2028**, the company itself owes a separate 21% tax: **$237,300**. Hensley buys the same company but nothing leaves it — no such tax. *(Small fourth item: the extra $250k price itself carries $18k of tax after basis differences. Total extra: $437,200.)*

**The bridge:** $250,000 (their bigger number) − $437,200 (their bigger tax) = **−$187,200** — before even counting that Hensley also pays you note interest (≈ $56,700 after tax) and takes all your liabilities off your plate.

Rule of thumb: **every $100 from Hensley → you keep $80. Every $100 of Lone Star's paper → you keep about $70.** Their premium adds 80¢ on the dollar; it can't dig out of a 30¢ hole.
