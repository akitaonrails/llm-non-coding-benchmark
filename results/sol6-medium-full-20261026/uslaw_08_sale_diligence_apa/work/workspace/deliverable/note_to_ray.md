# Ray — three things that could cost you after closing

**My recommendation: do not sign the buyer’s current draft.** A two-week *signing* is possible if the signed agreement first fixes the known installer exposure, the earn-out calculation, and the consent and permit conditions. The consents and new permit can be obtained between signing and closing if the agreement gives you a real right not to close without them. The present draft does not give you that protection.

1. **The nine 1099 installers.** The facts look like employees: Brightline sets their daily work, supplies trucks and tools, pays hourly, and has been their exclusive source of work for years. “Everyone does it” does not decide their status. The draft makes you promise classification was proper and lets Buyer claim up to the full purchase price. The nine received $612,000 in 2025, or $68,000 each on average ($612,000 ÷ 9); that is a payment figure, **not** an estimate of tax or wage liability. We need to disclose them in the schedule, remove the clean promise for these nine, and cap any specific pre-closing claim. Buyer must handle classification after closing. [Classification](../library/worker_classification_tx_irs.md); [caps](../library/baskets_caps.md).

2. **The $400,000 earn-out may pay very little.** Buyer’s draft lets Buyer calculate EBITDA under its own policies. Your 2025 seller-prepared EBITDA was $740,000, which would yield only $16,000 under the formula if repeated and accepted as the earn-out measure: 40% × ($740,000 − $700,000). If that figure includes the stated $180,000 salary and $42,000 legal add-backs, removing both gives $518,000 ($740,000 − $180,000 − $42,000), below the hurdle; we lack the underlying accounts to reconcile it. The $400,000 maximum needs $1,700,000 EBITDA ($700,000 + $400,000 ÷ 40%). We need a written calculation schedule, limits on Buyer charges, access to records, and a neutral accountant for disputes. [Earn-out drafting](../library/earnout_definitions.md).

3. **The escrow is not the same as a liability limit.** Buyer would hold $310,000 for 18 months. That is 10% of an implied $3.1 million price ($310,000 ÷ 10%); the full price is inferred from the draft, not otherwise verified. As written, the installer representation carries a full-price cap, and Buyer can claim even if it knew of the problem. We propose making the escrow the only source for ordinary and specifically capped installer claims, except fundamental-representation claims, and agreeing when installer reserves are released. The Hensley customer contract and shop lease also need written consents; Hensley can refuse in its sole discretion, so do not let the sale close as though its revenue will automatically transfer. Hensley is $1.748 million of reported revenue (38% × $4.6 million). [Caps](../library/baskets_caps.md); [sandbagging](../library/sandbagging.md); [assignment](../library/anti_assignment_change_of_control.md).

Buyer’s counsel calls the knowledge clause universal and §4.12 routine. The reference library recognizes both kinds of knowledge clause; “routine” wording does not make your representation true. Your installers’ working facts strongly indicate employee status under the library’s control test. A clean §4.12 promise plus the draft’s full-price cap would leave you exposed even after Buyer has read the data room. [Sandbagging](../library/sandbagging.md); [classification](../library/worker_classification_tx_irs.md); [caps](../library/baskets_caps.md).

**Before signing:** settle the contractor schedule and indemnity, the earn-out calculation, Hensley and landlord consent conditions, and the buyer’s plan for a new spray-booth permit. The permit is nontransferable and its stated reapplication time is 30–60 days; that may delay closing or booth operations, though it need not delay signing if the agreement has a workable closing condition.
